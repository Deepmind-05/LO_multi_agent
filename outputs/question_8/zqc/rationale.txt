

TARGET-LANGUAGE

Zoque (Copainalá) [zqc], Mixe-Zoque family, North America

CONSTRUCTION RATIONALE

1. **Establish the basic sentence architecture.** Design all puzzle sentences to follow VOS (Verb–Object–Subject) word order consistently. Every transitive sentence must have the form: [Verb] [NP₁] [NP₂], where NP₁ is the object (P) and NP₂ is the subject (A). Every intransitive sentence must have the form: [Verb] [NP], where the single NP is the intransitive subject (S). This positional regularity is the solver's first structural cue: the verb always comes first, and in transitive clauses the last NP is the agent. Do not include any sentence that deviates from this order. The solver should be able to identify the basic word order within the first few sentences without needing case marking as a clue.

2. **Design the case system (ergative-absolutive).** Create a single ergative case marker (a suffix or clitic on the NP) that appears ONLY on transitive subjects (A). Intransitive subjects (S) and transitive objects (P) share the same form (absolutive, which may be zero-marked or carry a distinct but consistent marker). The critical contrast the solver must see: the same noun, when it is an intransitive subject, has form X; when it is a transitive subject, has form X+ergative; when it is a transitive object, has form X (same as intransitive subject). Include at least three different nouns appearing in all three roles (S, A, P) so the solver can confirm the pattern is not noun-specific. The ergative marker must be phonologically distinct from any verbal affix to avoid confusion.

3. **Design the fused A/P person-number paradigm.** This is the central morphological system. Create a single set of verbal prefixes (or a single prefix slot) that encodes the A–P combination as a unit. The paradigm must include at minimum the following cells: 1sg–2sg, 1sg–3sg, 2sg–3sg, 3sg–3sg, 1sg–1pl.incl, 1sg–1pl.excl, 2sg–1pl.incl, 3sg–1pl.incl, 3sg–1pl.excl, plus intransitive cells: 1sg, 2sg, 3sg, 1pl.incl, 1pl.excl, 3pl. The critical design constraint: the transitive markers must NOT be decomposable into a predictable [A-marker] + [P-marker] concatenation. For example, if the intransitive 1sg marker is "ka-" and the intransitive 2sg marker is "tu-", the transitive 1sg–2sg marker must NOT be "ka-tu-" or "tu-ka-". It must be a unique, unpredictable form (e.g., "kwa-"). This is what makes the paradigm genuinely fused rather than two independent slots. The solver's task is to test the hypothesis "is this two slots or one?" by checking whether any transitive marker equals the concatenation of the corresponding intransitive markers.

4. **Ensure the verb's agreement follows accusative alignment.** The fused A/P marker must encode the person/number of BOTH arguments, but the system must be organized such that the P (object) value is the primary or "anchor" value in the paradigm. Concretely: the intransitive markers (1sg, 2sg, 3sg, etc.) should be recoverable as the "P-value" component of the transitive markers. For instance, the transitive 1sg–2sg marker should be morphologically related to the intransitive 2sg marker (the object's person), not the intransitive 1sg marker (the subject's person). This gives the solver a way to discover accusative alignment: the verb "looks like" it is agreeing with the object. However, because the marker is fused, the subject's person is also encoded (the marker for 1sg–2sg differs from 3sg–2sg, showing that the subject's person matters too). The solver must discover that BOTH arguments are encoded but that the system is organized around the object.

5. **Design the verb template with suffixal slots.** The full verb structure is: [Fused A/P prefix] + Root + [TAM suffix] + [Evidential suffix]. Design at least two TAM values (past and non-past) and at least two evidential values (direct/witnessed and indirect/hearsay). The TAM suffix and evidential suffix must be phonologically distinct from each other and from the roots. The evidential suffix must appear in a consistent position (after TAM). Include sentences where TAM varies while evidential is held constant, and sentences where evidential varies while TAM is held constant, so the solver can isolate each slot. The evidential should be semantically motivated in the translations (e.g., "I heard that..." or "apparently..." for indirect; direct assertion for witnessed).

6. **Design the inclusive/exclusive distinction.** The 1pl value must have two distinct forms throughout the paradigm: one for inclusive (speaker + addressee) and one for exclusive (speaker + others). This applies both to intransitive markers (1pl.incl vs. 1pl.excl as S) and to transitive markers (where 1pl can be A or P). The translations must provide contextual evidence: use second-person plural address ("you all") in sentences with the inclusive form, and first-person plural reference to a group not including the addressee in sentences with the exclusive form. Include at least two sentences with 1pl.incl and two with 1pl.excl in different A/P/S roles so the solver can confirm the distinction is consistent across positions.

7. **Generate the data set (20–28 sentences).** Systematically combine the elements to ensure:
   - At least 4 different verb roots appear in transitive contexts with different A–P combinations (to demonstrate the fused paradigm is not root-specific).
   - At least 3 different verb roots appear in intransitive contexts (to show the intransitive paradigm).
   - At least 3 different nouns appear in all three grammatical roles (S, A, P) to establish the case system.
   - The ergative marker appears on at least 4 different nouns in the A position.
   - At least 2 sentences with indirect evidential and at least 2 with direct (zero or marked) evidential.
   - At least 2 sentences with past TAM and at least 2 with non-past TAM.
   - At least 2 sentences with 1pl.incl and at least 2 with 1pl.excl.
   - At least one sentence where the subject is 3sg and the object is 1sg (to show that the verb marker changes with the subject even when the object is fixed, confirming the subject is encoded in the fused slot).
   - At least one sentence where the object is 3sg and the subject is 1sg vs. 2sg (to show the object is encoded, confirming accusative organization).
   - Present all data as sentences with English translations only. Do not provide morpheme glosses, brackets, or any explicit segmentation.

8. **Introduce one morpheme-boundary-ambiguous verb.** Select one transitive verb whose root begins with a segment that is identical to (or a minimal variant of) the final segment of one of the fused A/P markers. For example, if the 1sg–3sg fused marker ends in "-n", choose a root that begins with "n-". In the data, show this verb ONLY with the 1sg–3sg A–P combination (so the solver sees [marker ending in -n] + [root beginning with n-] as a continuous string). Never show this verb with any other A–P combination. This creates genuine ambiguity: the solver cannot determine whether the boundary falls before or after the "n". The solver must use paradigmatic evidence (other verbs with the same A–P combination, or the same verb in a different combination if one is provided in a question) to resolve this.

9. **Verify the data set for unintended ambiguities and contradictions.** Check that: (a) no two different A–P combinations produce the same fused marker (the paradigm must be non-syncretic); (b) the ergative case marker does not accidentally match any verbal affix; (c) the TAM and evidential suffixes do not accidentally match any root-final segment in a way that creates false segmentation; (d) the inclusive/exclusive forms are never confused with each other in the data; (e) the VOS order is unambiguous in every sentence (no sentence could be reanalyzed as having a different word order); (f) the boundary-ambiguous verb's ambiguity is the ONLY genuine ambiguity in the data.

10. **Design Question 1: Identify the case system and word order.** Ask the solver to: (a) state the basic word order of the language; (b) identify which NP in a transitive sentence is the subject and which is the object, and explain the evidence; (c) identify the ergative marker and state the rule for when it appears. This question tests the solver's ability to extract the case system and word order from the data. It should be solvable from the positional pattern (VOS) and the contrast between transitive and intransitive sentences. The solver should be able to answer this before fully analyzing the verb morphology.

11. **Design Question 2: Analyze the verb's person-number system.** Ask the solver to: (a) list all distinct verbal person-number markers observed in the data; (b) determine whether the transitive markers are composed of two independent slots (one for subject, one for object) or a single fused slot, and provide evidence for the answer; (c) determine which argument (subject or object) the verbal system is organized around, and provide evidence. This is the core analytical question. The solver must perform the decomposability test (checking whether transitive markers = concatenation of intransitive markers) and the alignment test (checking which argument's person/number is the "anchor"). The evidence for (b) is the non-decomposability of the fused markers. The evidence for (c) is the morphological relationship between transitive markers and the intransitive marker of the object (not the subject).

12. **Design Question 3: Parse novel sentences.** Provide 4–5 novel sentences (not in the data) that the solver must fully parse into morphemes and translate. Choose sentences that: (a) use a verb root seen in the data but with an A–P combination not seen in the data (testing whether the solver can identify the correct fused marker from the paradigm); (b) use a noun in a grammatical role not seen in the data (testing case assignment); (c) include an indirect evidential with a TAM value not previously seen with that verb (testing slot identification); (d) include a 1pl.incl or 1pl.excl in a role not previously seen (testing whether the solver correctly identified the inclusive/exclusive distinction); (e) include the boundary-ambiguous verb in a NEW A–P combination (testing whether the solver can resolve the boundary ambiguity using the new context). Accept morpheme-boundary notation where the solver is uncertain.

13. **Design Question 4: Construct sentences.** Provide 5 English sentences that the solver must translate into the target language. Choose sentences that: (a) require a transitive verb with an A–P combination not directly attested in the data (testing productive use of the fused paradigm); (b) require an intransitive verb with a TAM value not seen with that verb (testing slot combination); (c) require the ergative case on a noun not previously seen in the A position (testing case generalization); (d) require 1pl.excl as the object (testing whether the solver correctly assigns the exclusive form in the P position); (e) require an indirect evidential with a past TAM (testing suffix ordering). The solver must produce the full surface form of the verb, including the correct fused A/P marker, root, TAM suffix, and evidential suffix in the correct order.

14. **Design Question 5: Metalinguistic explanation of split alignment.** Ask the solver to explain why the case system and the verbal agreement system appear to "disagree" about which argument is special. Specifically: the case system marks the transitive subject (ergative), while the verbal system is organized around the object (accusative). The solver must articulate that these are two independent systems with different alignment patterns, and explain what evidence in the data supports this conclusion. This question tests whether the solver has integrated the two systems rather than treating them as one. The expected answer should note: (a) the ergative marker appears on the transitive subject in the NP; (b) the verbal marker is organized around the object's person/number; (c) these two facts are independent (the verb does not simply "copy" the case marking).

15. **Design Question 6: Resolve the boundary ambiguity.** Ask the solver to identify the verb in the data whose root is ambiguous with respect to the morpheme boundary, explain why it is ambiguous, and provide the correct segmentation. The solver should be able to resolve this by: (a) noting that the verb only appears with one A–P combination in the data; (b) using the fact that the fused marker for that combination is known from other verbs; (c) subtracting the known marker from the surface string to recover the root. If the boundary-ambiguous verb appears in a novel combination in Question 3, the solver can use that form to confirm the root.

16. **Calibrate difficulty progression.** The questions should be ordered so that each builds on the previous: Q1 (case + word order) is the simplest and provides the structural framework. Q2 (verb analysis) is the core analytical challenge and requires the solver to perform a structural test. Q3 (parsing) requires productive application of all discovered rules. Q4 (construction) requires the solver to generate novel forms, which is harder than parsing because the solver must select the correct fused marker from memory of the paradigm. Q5 (metalinguistic) requires integration and articulation. Q6 (ambiguity resolution) requires the solver to identify a specific problem in the data and solve it using paradigmatic reasoning. Each question should be solvable only if the solver has correctly induced the relevant rules, but the questions test different cognitive operations: identification, analysis, parsing, production, explanation, and problem-solving.

17. **Final validation.** Re-solve the entire problem from the solver's perspective using ONLY the provided data. Confirm that: (a) the VOS word order is unambiguous from the data alone; (b) the ergative marker is uniquely identifiable (no other morpheme could be reanalyzed as the ergative); (c) the fused A/P paradigm is uniquely determined (no alternative slot analysis fits the data); (d) the accusative alignment of the verb is uniquely supported (the evidence for object-anchoring is stronger than any evidence for subject-anchoring); (e) the inclusive/exclusive distinction is uniquely supported by the contextual evidence; (f) the evidential slot is uniquely identifiable (no other suffix could be reanalyzed as the evidential); (g) the boundary-ambiguous verb has exactly two possible parses and the correct one is uniquely determined by the available evidence; (h) no question has an unintended alternative answer; (i) the total number of distinct morphemes the solver must identify is manageable (aim for 25–40 distinct morphemes total across the puzzle).
