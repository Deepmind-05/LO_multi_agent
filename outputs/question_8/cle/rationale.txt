

TARGET-LANGUAGE: Lealao Chinantec (cle), Oto-Manguean

CONSTRUCTION RATIONALE

1. **Define the core sentence template and verb morphology.** Establish that the basic clause order is Verb–Object–Subject (VOS). The verb is a simple form consisting of at most two or three morphemes: a subject person/number prefix (or clitic) followed by a verbal root, optionally followed by a small aspect suffix. There is NO object marking on the verb. The verb is not agglutinative; it should never exceed three morphemes. This constraint (reduced verbal morphology) is a design principle: it keeps the verb simple so that the central puzzle challenge is syntactic (word order + agreement) rather than morphological (segmenting a long string). The solver's primary task will be to identify which of two NPs is the subject, not to parse a complex verb.

2. **Design the subject person/number marker system with deliberate syncretism.** Create a set of verbal subject markers for at least 1SG, 2SG, 3SG, 1PL, 2PL, and 3PL. All markers must be overt (no zero forms). Introduce syncretism in at least one pair: for example, 1SG and 1PL share the same marker, or 2SG and 2PL share the same marker. At least one person/number value must have a unique marker (e.g., 3PL distinct from 3SG) so that the system is not fully syncretic and the solver can still make progress in many cases. The syncretic pair will be the source of the puzzle's central ambiguity: when the verbal marker is syncretic, agreement alone cannot identify the subject's number, and the solver must use an independent pronoun or semantic context to resolve it.

3. **Design the aspect system with a clear suppletive vs. regular contrast.** Create at least three aspect values (e.g., perfective, imperfective, and a third such as completive or habitual). For at least two verbs, implement one aspect value via suppletion: the verb root itself changes entirely (e.g., "eat" has root *kɔ* in the imperfective but root *tɛ* in the perfective). For at least one other verb, implement the same aspect value via a regular affix (e.g., a suffix *-n* added to the root). The solver must compare across verbs to distinguish these two strategies: if a verb's root changes entirely for a given aspect, it is suppletive; if the root stays the same and an affix appears, it is regular. This contrast is the target-language counterpart of the source's "irregular verb" concept, but it is systematic (applies to multiple verbs) rather than idiosyncratic (one verb). The solver's task is to identify which verbs are suppletive and which are regular, and to predict the correct form for a novel verb/aspect combination.

4. **Design the independent pronoun system.** Create a set of independent pronouns (used as NPs in the sentence) for the same person/number values. Critically, the pronoun system must be MORE fine-grained than the verbal agreement system in at least one dimension. For example, if the verb is syncretic for 1SG/1PL, the independent pronouns must distinguish them (e.g., *a* for 1SG, *aʔ* or *a-w* for 1PL). This creates the resolution mechanism for syncretic verbal markers: when the verb cannot distinguish 1SG from 1PL, the independent pronoun in the subject position can. The solver must discover that the pronoun carries information the verb does not, and learn to use both cues together.

5. **(Optional) Introduce tone as a morphologically relevant feature.** If including tone, mark it clearly in the orthography (diacritics or tone numbers). Use tone in at least one of the following ways: (a) two suppletive roots that are segmentally identical but differ in tone (e.g., *kɔ* with high tone = imperfective, *kɔ* with low tone = perfective); or (b) a person/number marker that has a tone variant in a specific context. The tone contrast must be demonstrated in the data with at least one minimal pair (two forms identical in segments but different in tone, with different meanings). The solver must recognize that tone is part of the morphological signal, not just phonetic variation. This adds a layer that solvers focused only on segmental content may initially miss.

6. **(Optional) Include the inclusive/exclusive 1PL asymmetry.** If included, the independent pronouns must distinguish 1PL inclusive (speaker + addressee) from 1PL exclusive (speaker + others), but the verbal agreement must use a single 1PL marker for both. The data must contain at least one clause with an inclusive 1PL subject and one with an exclusive 1PL subject, both showing the same verbal marker but different pronouns. The solver must notice that the verb cannot distinguish these two values and that the pronoun is the sole carrier of this distinction.

7. **Generate the data set as full sentences in VOS order.** Present 12–18 sentences, each in the form [Verb] [Object NP] [Subject NP], with English translations. Do NOT provide morpheme glosses, brackets, or labels. The data must satisfy the following coverage requirements:
   - At least 4–5 transitive clauses with unambiguous subject identification (the verbal marker clearly matches one NP and not the other, e.g., a 1SG marker with a 1SG pronoun subject and a 3SG object).
   - At least 2 transitive clauses with 3SG subjects (testing the overt 3SG marker).
   - At least 2 transitive clauses with syncretic subject markers (e.g., 1PL subject where the marker is identical to 1SG), each accompanied by an independent pronoun that resolves the ambiguity.
   - At least 2 intransitive clauses (verb + subject only, no object NP) to confirm that intransitives simply lack the object slot.
   - At least 2 verbs showing suppletive aspect (the root changes for a particular aspect), visible across at least 2 different person/number values each.
   - At least 1 verb showing regular aspect marking (same root + affix) for the same aspect value, providing the contrast.
   - At least 1 negative sentence (if including the negative particle) showing the preverbal position: [Neg] [Verb] [Object] [Subject].
   - If tone is included: at least one minimal pair demonstrating the tone contrast.
   - If inclusive/exclusive is included: at least one inclusive and one exclusive 1PL subject clause.

8. **Arrange the data for progressive discovery.** Order the sentences (or present them in a way that) supports a natural discovery sequence:
   - **Stage 1 (VOS + subject agreement):** The first several sentences should have unambiguous subject identification. The verbal marker clearly matches one NP (e.g., a 1SG marker, and one NP is a 1SG pronoun). The solver should be able to infer: (a) the first element is a verb (it changes form, has person markers), (b) the verbal marker identifies the subject, (c) therefore the final NP is the subject and the middle NP is the object. This is the central "aha" moment.
   - **Stage 2 (Aspect system):** Subsequent sentences should show the same verb in different aspects, making the aspect marking visible. The solver should notice that some verbs change their root (suppletive) while others add an affix (regular). This requires comparing across verbs.
   - **Stage 3 (Syncretic ambiguity):** Later sentences should present syncretic cases where the verbal marker is ambiguous. The solver must now use the independent pronoun to resolve the subject's number. This teaches the solver that agreement is necessary but not always sufficient.
   - **Stage 4 (Optional layers):** Tone contrasts and inclusive/exclusive distinctions appear in the later data, adding further complexity.

9. **Verify the data set for unintended ambiguities and solvability.** Check that: (a) no sentence has a parse where the verbal marker could match BOTH NPs (unless that is the intended syncretic case); (b) the suppletive roots are phonologically distinct from the regular roots and from the person markers (no accidental mergers); (c) the intransitive sentences are clearly distinguishable from transitive ones (fewer NPs); (d) the negative particle (if included) is not confusable with any person marker or aspect marker; (e) every rule needed for the questions is uniquely inducible from the data; (f) no question has an unintended answer.

10. **Design Question 1: Parse novel sentences (Target → English).** Provide 3–4 novel sentences (not in the data) that the solver must translate into English. Choose sentences that test:
    - (a) A transitive clause with a person/number combination not seen in the data (e.g., 2PL subject) but using a verb and aspect seen in the data. This tests whether the solver has generalized the VOS + agreement pattern.
    - (b) A transitive clause using a suppletive verb in an aspect not directly attested for that specific person/number combination. The solver must predict the suppletive root from the pattern.
    - (c) A syncretic case (e.g., 1PL subject) where the solver must use the independent pronoun to determine the correct English translation (e.g., "we (incl.)" vs. "I").
    - (d) (If tone is included) A form where the tone on the root determines the aspect, testing whether the solver recognized tone as morphologically relevant.
    Each sentence should require the solver to combine multiple pieces of knowledge (word order + agreement + aspect + possibly tone) rather than simply copying a form from the data.

11. **Design Question 2: Construct sentences (English → Target).** Provide 4–5 English sentences that the solver must translate into the target language. Choose sentences that test:
    - (a) A transitive clause requiring the solver to assemble the correct VOS order, select the correct subject marker, and choose the correct aspect form (regular or suppletive).
    - (b) A clause with a 3PL subject (testing the unique 3PL marker, distinct from 3SG).
    - (c) A clause with a syncretic subject (e.g., 1PL) where the solver must choose the correct independent pronoun (distinguishing inclusive from exclusive if that concept is included).
    - (d) An intransitive clause (testing that the solver knows intransitives simply omit the object NP).
    - (e) (If negative is included) A negative sentence (testing the preverbal position of the negative particle).
    The solver must produce the full sentence, not just the verb form. This tests productive knowledge of the entire system.

12. **Design Question 3: Metalinguistic analysis of the aspect system.** Ask the solver to: (a) identify which verbs in the data are suppletive (change their root for a particular aspect) and which are regular (add an affix); (b) explain the difference between the two strategies; (c) predict the form of a novel verb in a specified aspect, given that the solver is told whether the novel verb is suppletive or regular. This tests whether the solver has abstracted the aspect system as a typological distinction rather than memorizing individual forms. The question should require the solver to articulate a generalization, not just identify specific forms.

13. **Design Question 4: Resolve syncretic ambiguity.** Present a specific sentence (or a pair of sentences) where the verbal marker is syncretic and the solver must determine the correct interpretation. The sentence should be resolvable ONLY by using the independent pronoun (or, in one case, by semantic plausibility if no pronoun is present). Ask the solver to: (a) provide the correct English translation; (b) explain why the verbal marker alone is insufficient; (c) identify what additional cue resolves the ambiguity. This tests the solver's understanding that the verbal agreement system is underspecified relative to the pronominal system, and that multi-cue reasoning is required.

14. **(Optional) Design Question 5: Tone or inclusive/exclusive.** If tone is included, ask the solver to identify the tone contrast in the data and explain its morphological function (e.g., "the tone on the root distinguishes perfective from imperfective for verb X"). If inclusive/exclusive is included, ask the solver to explain why the verb cannot distinguish the two 1PL values and what carries the distinction instead. This tests the solver's ability to identify a systematic asymmetry between two subsystems.

15. **Calibrate difficulty progression.** Ensure the questions form a clear difficulty gradient:
    - Q1 (Parsing) requires pattern recognition and application of discovered rules to novel combinations. The solver must correctly identify word order, agreement, and aspect.
    - Q2 (Construction) requires productive assembly of the full sentence, testing all components simultaneously.
    - Q3 (Metalinguistic) requires abstracting a generalization about the aspect system and applying it to a novel verb.
    - Q4 (Ambiguity resolution) requires recognizing a limitation in the verbal system and using a secondary cue.
    - Q5 (Optional) requires identifying a cross-subsystem asymmetry.
    Each question should be solvable only if the solver has correctly induced the relevant rules from the data, but each tests a different cognitive operation: application, production, generalization, multi-cue integration, and metalinguistic comparison.

16. **Final validation: Re-solve from the solver's perspective.** Using ONLY the provided data (no external knowledge of Chinantec, Oto-Manguean, or Mesoamerican languages), verify that: (a) the VOS pattern is uniquely inducible from the first several sentences; (b) the subject agreement pattern is unambiguous in the early data; (c) the suppletive vs. regular aspect distinction is clearly visible from comparing at least two verbs; (d) the syncretic cases are resolvable using the independent pronoun; (e) no question has an unintended answer; (f) the tone contrasts (if included) are clearly marked and demonstrable; (g) the inclusive/exclusive distinction (if included) is visible in the data; (h) the overall puzzle is solvable through general linguistic reasoning alone, without any prior knowledge of the target language.
