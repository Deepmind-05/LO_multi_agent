

TARGET-LANGUAGE

Tukang Besi (Austronesian, Celebic, Sulawesi)

CONSTRUCTION RATIONALE

1. **Establish the verbal morphological template.** Define the verb as a prefixing complex with the following slots in fixed order: TAM prefix + Subject prefix + Object prefix + Root. The person/number system is 1, 2, 3 × singular, plural (six values). The subject prefix set marks both intransitive subjects (S) and transitive subjects (A) — this is accusative alignment in the verbal domain. The object prefix set marks only transitive objects (O); intransitive verbs simply omit the object slot. Choose prefix forms that are phonologically distinct from each other and from the roots, with the exception of one deliberately chosen root (see Step 6). The TAM slot is occupied by a future prefix; the non-future (past) form is unmarked (zero in the TAM slot). This step establishes the basic architecture the solver must discover. The solver should be able to infer the template order and the function of each slot from the distribution of prefixes across intransitive and transitive clauses.

2. **Establish the nominal alignment system.** Define a simple case system: transitive subjects (A) are marked with an ergative prefix or suffix on the NP; intransitive subjects (S) and transitive objects (O) are unmarked (absolutive). Nouns have NO plural marking — number is not morphologically encoded on the noun. This means the only morphological signal of transitivity in the nominal domain is the ergative marker. This step is critical because it creates the central contrast: the ergative NP marker marks A, but the verbal subject prefix marks both S and A. The solver must discover both systems independently before recognizing their divergence. The absence of nominal plural marking also makes the verbal agreement system more prominent, since it is the primary morphological encoding of number for full NPs.

3. **Choose 10–14 semantically diverse verb roots.** Include a mix of transitive verbs (e.g., chop, steal, bury, hang, overtake, listen, love, bite, fill, tie) and intransitive verbs (e.g., go, grow, be sick, sleep). Ensure roots are phonologically distinct from the subject and object prefixes to avoid accidental mergers, EXCEPT for one deliberately chosen root (see Step 6). The roots should be short (1–3 syllables) to reflect the simple syllable structure of the language. The phonological inventory should avoid voicing contrasts and may include implosives or other distinctive features to make the data look typologically plausible without adding puzzle-relevant complexity.

4. **Design one suppletive verb with tense-based root alternation.** Select one verb (preferably intransitive, e.g., "go" or "sleep") whose root changes form depending on tense: one root appears in the future (marked) form, a different root appears in the non-future (unmarked) form. This is distinct from the source rationale's person/number-based alternation — here the alternation is tied to the TAM contrast. Ensure the alternation is visible in at least three data items (e.g., the verb appears in future with two different subjects, and in non-future with one subject, or some similar distribution). Do NOT gloss the alternation as irregular; the solver must discover it. The suppletive verb should be the ONLY verb showing this property, so the solver can identify it by contrast with regular verbs that maintain a stable root across tenses.

5. **Design one morpheme-boundary-ambiguous verb.** Choose a transitive verb whose root begins with a segment (or segment sequence) identical to or minimally variant with one of the object prefixes. For example, if the 3rd plural object prefix is "na-", choose a root like "nata" so that the string "na+nata" could be parsed as prefix "na-" + root "nata" OR as a fused sequence where the boundary is elsewhere. Ensure the data shows this verb ONLY with the object value that creates the ambiguity (e.g., only with 3rd plural object), and NEVER with a different object value that would resolve the ambiguity. This creates genuine underdetermination: the solver cannot determine the root from the data alone without additional paradigmatic evidence. If you include reduplication (see Step 8), the reduplicated form of this verb can serve as the paradigmatic evidence that resolves the ambiguity.

6. **Generate the data set (20–28 full clauses).** Present the data as complete sentences with English translations only (no morpheme glosses, no brackets). Each clause must include the full NP(s) and the verb. Systematically combine elements to ensure:
   - At least 4 intransitive clauses showing the subject NP unmarked (absolutive) and the verbal subject prefix, with no object prefix.
   - At least 4 transitive clauses showing the subject NP marked with ergative, the object NP unmarked (absolutive), and both verbal subject and object prefixes.
   - Each subject prefix value appears with at least 2 different roots.
   - Each object prefix value (including zero for intransitives) appears with at least 2 different roots.
   - The future prefix appears with at least 3 different roots; the unmarked (non-future) form appears with at least 3 different roots.
   - The suppletive verb appears in at least 3 forms showing the root alternation across the future/non-future contrast.
   - At least 2 regular verbs appear in BOTH future and non-future forms, maintaining the same root (to contrast with the suppletive verb).
   - The ambiguous verb appears ONLY with the object value that creates segmentation ambiguity.
   - The ergative NP marker appears in at least 3 transitive clauses (so the solver can identify it as a consistent marker).
   - The intransitive subject NP is unmarked in at least 3 intransitive clauses (so the solver can identify the absolutive as the unmarked case).
   - No noun in the data shows any plural morphology (reinforcing the absence of nominal plural marking).

7. **Verify the data set for unintended ambiguities and solvability.** Check that: (a) the ergative marker is unambiguously identifiable — it appears only on transitive subjects and never on intransitive subjects or transitive objects; (b) the verbal subject prefix is unambiguously identifiable — it appears on both intransitive and transitive subjects, and its form is consistent regardless of whether the clause is transitive or intransitive; (c) the future prefix is unambiguously identifiable — it appears only in future clauses and is absent in non-future clauses; (d) the suppletive verb's alternation cannot be reanalyzed as two different regular verbs (the English translations must make clear it is the same lexical item); (e) the ambiguous verb's two parses are the ONLY two possible parses given the data; (f) no root accidentally matches a prefix in a way that creates an unintended segmentation problem; (g) the absence of nominal plural is noticeable but not confusing (the solver should not think a missing plural marker is an error).

8. **Optionally include reduplication.** If the puzzle has room for additional complexity, include 2–3 reduplicated verb forms (full or partial reduplication) with a transparent semantic function (e.g., iterative: "chop" → "chop repeatedly"). The reduplicated forms should appear in the data with English translations that make the iterative/distributive meaning clear. If the ambiguous verb (Step 5) is reduplicated in one of the data items, the reduplicated form can serve as paradigmatic evidence to resolve the segmentation ambiguity (the reduplicated copy reveals the true root boundary). If reduplication is included, ensure it does not create additional unintended ambiguities and that its function is inferable from the contrast between base and reduplicated forms.

9. **Design Question 1 (Parsing: Target → English).** Provide 3–4 novel full clauses (not in the data) that require the solver to decompose both the nominal and verbal morphology. Choose clauses that: (a) combine a transitive clause with a subject and object person/number combination not directly attested in the data (testing generalization of the agreement paradigm); (b) use the suppletive verb in a tense not directly attested for that verb (testing whether the solver generalized the alternation pattern); (c) use an intransitive verb with the future prefix (testing whether the solver correctly identifies the unmarked form as non-future and the marked form as future). Accept full English translations.

10. **Design Question 2 (Construction: English → Target).** Provide 4–5 English sentences requiring the solver to build full clauses. Choose sentences that: (a) require a transitive clause with a specific ergative-marked subject and unmarked object (testing whether the solver correctly applies the nominal alignment); (b) require the suppletive verb in a tense not directly attested (testing generalization of the root alternation); (c) require an intransitive clause with a specific subject (testing whether the solver correctly leaves the subject NP unmarked and uses the correct verbal subject prefix); (d) require a transitive clause with the future prefix (testing full template assembly: future prefix + subject prefix + object prefix + root, plus correct nominal case marking). Accept morpheme-boundary notation where the solver may be uncertain about exact phonological forms at boundaries.

11. **Design Question 3 (Metalinguistic: Identify the split alignment).** Ask the solver to explain the relationship between the case marking on NPs and the person/number marking on the verb. Specifically, ask: "In this language, the same participant (the transitive subject) is marked differently in the noun phrase versus on the verb. Describe both patterns and explain how they differ." This is the central question of the puzzle. The solver must: (a) identify that the ergative NP marker marks only transitive subjects; (b) identify that the verbal subject prefix marks both intransitive and transitive subjects; (c) articulate that these are two different alignment patterns (ergative-absolutive in the nominal domain, accusative in the verbal domain). This question is solvable only if the solver has correctly induced both alignment systems from the data and recognized their contrast.

12. **Design Question 4 (Metalinguistic: Identify the suppletive verb).** Ask the solver to identify which verb in the data set does not maintain a stable root across tenses, and to provide its English translation and the two root forms. This tests whether the solver noticed the root alternation and can articulate why it deviates from the regular pattern (where all other verbs maintain the same root in both future and non-future forms). Ensure only ONE verb in the data set shows this property.

13. **Design Question 5 (Ambiguity: Identify multiple valid analyses).** Ask the solver to provide all possible target-language forms for a specific English sentence involving the ambiguous verb with a DIFFERENT object value than the one attested in the data (e.g., if the data only shows the verb with 3rd plural object, ask for the form with 1st singular object). The solver must recognize that the data does not uniquely determine the root of this verb and provide both possible forms (differing in whether the ambiguous segment is part of the root or part of the object prefix). If reduplication was included and provides resolving evidence, the solver may be expected to use it to select the correct analysis — in this case, the question should ask the solver to explain which analysis is supported by the reduplicated form and why.

14. **Calibrate difficulty progression.** Ensure the questions test a clear progression of cognitive operations: Q1 requires pattern recognition and productive parsing (applying discovered rules to new combinations); Q2 requires productive construction (building forms from scratch, including correct nominal case marking); Q3 requires metalinguistic comparison and synthesis (identifying and articulating the contrast between two alignment systems — the hardest inference); Q4 requires metalinguistic identification (spotting deviation from a regular pattern); Q5 requires awareness of underdetermination (recognizing that the evidence is insufficient to choose between two analyses, or using additional evidence to resolve it). Each question should be solvable only if the solver has correctly induced the relevant rules from the data, but the questions should test different cognitive operations on those rules. The split alignment (Q3) should be the most challenging question, as it requires integrating discoveries from multiple data types (nominal and verbal) into a single insight.

15. **Final validation.** Re-solve the entire problem from the solver's perspective using ONLY the provided data. Confirm that: (a) every rule needed to answer every question is uniquely inducible from the data; (b) the ergative marker is identifiable without ambiguity (it never appears on intransitive subjects or transitive objects); (c) the verbal alignment (accusative) is identifiable without ambiguity (the subject prefix is the same form whether the clause is transitive or intransitive); (d) the split alignment contrast is the ONLY explanation that accounts for both the nominal and verbal patterns; (e) the future prefix is identifiable and the unmarked form is interpretable as non-future; (f) the suppletive verb's alternation is generalizable to the tense tested in Q2; (g) the ambiguous verb's two parses are the ONLY two possible parses given the data (or, if reduplication resolves it, the resolution is unambiguous); (h) no question has an unintended third or fourth answer; (i) the absence of nominal plural marking is noticeable but does not create confusion about the case system.
