

TARGET-LANGUAGE: Hmong Njua (Hmong-Mien, Hmongic)

CONSTRUCTION RATIONALE

1. **Establish the controlled vocabulary and morphological constraints.** Select 8–12 content words spanning: 3–4 intransitive verbs, 2–3 transitive verbs, 4–5 common nouns, 2–3 proper names or 3rd-person pronouns, 2–3 adjectives, 1–2 demonstratives, and 2–3 numerals. All nouns must be countable and semantically unambiguous in their intended translations. Critically, all lexical items must be monomorphemic or at least appear as fixed, unanalyzable forms. Do not introduce any inflectional morphology, case markers, verbal agreement, gender distinctions, or plural suffixes. The language is exclusively isolating: every grammatical relation is expressed by a separate word or by position. If tone diacritics appear in the orthography, treat them as part of the lexical form (like spelling) and do not make them a feature the solver must analyze.

2. **Design the preverbal particle system (TAM + Negation).** Define 2–3 TAM particles (e.g., a perfective/completive marker, a future marker, and a zero form for present/habitual) that appear as separate words immediately before the verb. Define one negation particle that also appears in the preverbal zone. Specify the fixed order of preverbal elements: Subject – TAM – Negation – Verb (or Subject – Negation – TAM – Verb; choose one and be consistent). Ensure all preverbal particles are lexically distinct from each other and from any content word. This creates the "preverbal slot" that the solver must parse: the zone between the subject and the verb may contain zero, one, or two particles in a fixed order.

3. **Design the noun phrase structure.** Specify that adjectives and demonstratives follow the head noun (Noun–Adjective, Noun–Demonstrative). Specify that numerals precede the noun (Numeral–Noun), making this the one exception to the right-branching pattern. Nouns have no plural morphology; number is expressed only by numerals or context. The numeral "one" may function as an indefinite article (a/an). This right-branching pattern is the key NP-internal rule the solver must discover, and the numeral exception sharpens it by showing that the pattern is not simply "everything follows the noun."

4. **Design the relative clause system.** Specify that relative clauses are postnominal: the RC follows the head noun directly. There is no dedicated relative clause marker or complementizer. Relativization on the subject uses a gap strategy: the subject position in the RC is simply empty, and the head noun "fills" that gap. The RC is a finite clause with its own verb (and optionally its own TAM particles). If the RC's internal verb is transitive, the object appears in the RC in its normal postverbal position. Do not introduce a relative clause marker; the gap is the only signal.

5. **Design the interrogative/indefinite system.** Select 2–3 interrogative pronouns (e.g., 'who,' 'what,' 'where'). Specify that these same forms (or closely related forms) also function as indefinite pronouns ('someone,' 'something,' 'somewhere') in non-interrogative contexts. The distinction between interrogative and indefinite use is signaled by the presence or absence of a question particle. The question particle is a separate word that appears in a non-initial position (e.g., clause-final or post-verbal). In statements, the interrogative form appears without the question particle and receives an indefinite reading.

6. **Compose the data set — Layer 1: Simple intransitive sentences.** Provide 3–4 sentences with intransitive verbs, a single preverbal subject (a pronoun or proper name), and varying TAM particles (including the zero form). These sentences should allow the solver to induce: (a) the basic word order is Subject–Verb (the preverbal NP is the subject); (b) preverbal particles that are not the subject are TAM markers; (c) nouns do not change form for number (the same noun form appears whether the context implies one or many). Include at least one sentence where the subject is a full NP with an adjective or demonstrative following the noun, so the solver begins to see the right-branching NP pattern.

7. **Compose the data set — Layer 2: Simple transitive sentences.** Provide 3–4 sentences with transitive verbs, a preverbal subject, and a postverbal object. These sentences should allow the solver to induce: (a) the full SVO order (Subject–Verb–Object); (b) the postverbal NP is the object (confirming SVO rather than SOV or other orders); (c) the verb does not agree with any argument (the same verb form appears regardless of subject or object). Include at least one sentence where the object is a full NP with a postnominal modifier (adjective or demonstrative), reinforcing the right-branching NP pattern. Include at least one sentence with a numeral preceding the noun (e.g., "three birds") to introduce the Numeral–Noun order as an exception to the right-branching pattern.

8. **Compose the data set — Layer 3: Negation and TAM co-occurrence.** Provide 2–3 negated sentences. Include at least one sentence with both a TAM particle and the negation particle, so the solver can observe their relative order in the preverbal zone. Include at least one negated sentence with a transitive verb so the solver can confirm that negation does not change the SVO order (the negation particle appears between subject and verb, not after the verb or at the end). The solver should be able to induce: (a) the negation particle's position (between subject and verb); (b) the fixed order of TAM and negation when both are present; (c) that negation is a separate word, not a verbal prefix.

9. **Compose the data set — Layer 4: Postnominal relative clauses.** Provide 3–4 sentences containing relative clauses. Include: (a) an RC modifying the object of a transitive verb (e.g., "the bird that the child saw"); (b) an RC modifying the subject of a transitive verb; (c) an RC modifying the subject of an intransitive verb. In each case, the RC follows the head noun directly with no marker. The RC contains a gap in the subject position (for subject relativization). To make the gap discoverable, include at least one pair where the RC content is also presented (or easily reconstructable) as a standalone clause, so the solver can compare and identify the missing element. The solver should induce: (a) a clause following a noun is a modifier of that noun, not a separate clause; (b) the gap in the RC corresponds to the head noun; (c) the RC is a finite clause with its own verb and (optionally) its own TAM.

10. **Compose the data set — Layer 5: Interrogatives and indefinites.** Provide 2–3 interrogative sentences (using the question particle in its non-initial position) and 2–3 declarative sentences using the same (or related) pronoun forms with an indefinite reading. The question particle should appear in a consistent non-initial position (e.g., after the verb or at the end of the clause). The solver should induce: (a) the question particle marks polar/wh-questions and appears in a specific position; (b) the interrogative pronoun forms also function as indefinites in statements; (c) the presence or absence of the question particle is what distinguishes the two uses. Include at least one sentence where an indefinite pronoun appears in the object position of a transitive verb, so the solver sees the indefinite form in a non-subject position.

11. **Compose the data set — Layer 6: Complex sentences combining multiple features.** Provide 2–3 sentences that combine: (a) a transitive main clause with SVO order; (b) a relative clause modifying one of the arguments (with a gap); (c) a TAM particle and/or negation in the main clause; (d) a right-branching NP (with adjective or demonstrative) on the head noun or inside the RC. At least one sentence should have an RC whose internal verb is transitive, creating a nested structure where the solver must identify both the main clause's SVO and the RC's internal SVO (with a gap). These sentences require the solver to apply all previously induced rules simultaneously.

12. **Verify the data set for unique inducibility.** Check the following: (a) SVO cannot be confused with SOV or VSO — ensure there are enough transitive sentences with unambiguous semantic roles (e.g., "the child sees the bird" vs. "the bird sees the child" using the same nouns in reversed positions) to confirm that the preverbal NP is the agent and the postverbal NP is the patient. (b) The right-branching NP pattern is clearly evidenced — ensure there are enough Noun–Adjective and Noun–Demonstrative examples, and that the Numeral–Noun exception is present to prevent the solver from overgeneralizing to "all modifiers follow the noun." (c) The preverbal zone is unambiguous — ensure the solver can distinguish the subject from TAM/negation particles (they should be lexically distinct, and the subject should always be a pronoun, proper name, or full NP, never a particle). (d) The gap in RCs is identifiable — ensure the RC content is simple enough that the solver can reconstruct the missing argument by comparing with a standalone clause or by semantic reasoning. (e) The interrogative/indefinite distinction is clear — ensure the question particle is unambiguously present in questions and absent in statements with indefinite pronouns. (f) No rule can be inferred from a single sentence alone — each rule should require at least two data points for confirmation.

13. **Design Question 1 (Translation to English): Progressive difficulty.** Create 3 sub-questions of increasing complexity: (a) a simple transitive sentence with a TAM particle and a right-branching NP on the object (tests SVO, TAM identification, NP segmentation); (b) a negated transitive sentence with a relative clause modifying the subject (tests negation position, RC identification, gap identification); (c) a complex sentence with a transitive main clause, a TAM particle, a relative clause modifying the object (with a transitive internal verb and its own gap), and a right-branching NP on the head noun (tests all features simultaneously).

14. **Design Question 2 (Translation to target language): Progressive difficulty.** Create 3–4 sub-questions: (a) a simple intransitive sentence with a specific TAM and a right-branching NP on the subject; (b) a transitive sentence with negation and a relative clause modifying the object; (c) an interrogative sentence using a wh-pronoun in the object position with the question particle; (d) a complex sentence combining a transitive main clause, a TAM particle, a relative clause with a gap, and an indefinite pronoun in the RC. Ensure each sub-question tests a combination of rules not directly present as a complete example in the data set.

15. **Design Question 3 (Explanation).** Require the solver to explicitly state the rules they have induced: (a) basic word order; (b) the function and position of preverbal particles (TAM and negation, and their relative order); (c) the noun phrase modifier order (including the numeral exception); (d) the relative clause structure (postnominal, gap strategy, no marker); (e) the interrogative/indefinite system (shared forms, question particle as the distinguishing cue); (f) the absence of plural marking and how number is expressed. This confirms the solver has correctly identified all rules rather than merely pattern-matching.

16. **Final ambiguity check on questions.** For each question, verify that the intended answer is the unique correct answer given the induced rules. Pay particular attention to: (a) relative clause attachment ambiguity — if a sentence could be parsed with the RC attaching to different nouns, either accept multiple readings or revise to eliminate the ambiguity; (b) the preverbal zone — ensure the solver cannot confuse a TAM particle with the subject or vice versa; (c) the gap in RCs — ensure the gap position is unambiguous given the SVO order (the missing element must be in the subject position of the RC, which is the preverbal position within the RC). For the most complex question, it is acceptable to accept multiple readings as long as the solver's explanation demonstrates understanding of the source of the ambiguity.
