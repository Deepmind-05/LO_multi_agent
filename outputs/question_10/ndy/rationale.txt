

TARGET-LANGUAGE: Ndyuka (Surinamese Creole, English-based)

CONSTRUCTION RATIONALE

1. **Define the grammatical parameter space for Ndyuka.** Establish the following as fixed properties of the language the puzzle will present: (a) basic SVO word order; (b) no morphological case marking — grammatical relations are determined solely by linear position relative to the verb; (c) tense expressed by independent preverbal particles (past, future, zero for present/habitual); (d) negation expressed by an independent preverbal particle; (e) obligatory overt subjects (no pro-drop); (f) a definite article "de" preceding the noun; (g) postnominal relative clauses using the gap strategy (no relative pronoun, no resumptive element); (h) postnominal demonstratives. These parameters are not stated to the solver; they must be induced from data.

2. **Choose a controlled vocabulary.** Select 8–12 content words spanning: (a) 3–4 intransitive verbs (e.g., 'run', 'sleep', 'fall', 'sing'); (b) 2–3 transitive verbs (e.g., 'see', 'eat', 'build'); (c) 4–5 common nouns (e.g., 'fish', 'child', 'house', 'tree', 'boat'); (d) 3rd-person pronouns (he, she, it, they) and at least one proper name; (e) 1–2 adverbs if desired (e.g., 'always', 'only'). Ensure semantic roles are unambiguous in intended translations. All nouns should be countable. The vocabulary should be small enough that the solver can track which words are which across sentences, but large enough to create meaningful contrasts.

3. **Design the preverbal zone architecture.** This is the central parsing challenge of the puzzle. The preverbal zone (everything before the main verb) can contain, in order: [Subject] [Tense particle] [Negation particle] [Verb]. The subject is always a full pronoun or NP. The tense particle is optional (zero for present/habitual). The negation particle is optional. The solver must discover: (a) which preverbal element is the subject (it varies across sentences, is a pronoun or NP, and is semantically the agent/experiencer); (b) which is the tense particle (it is constant across sentences with the same temporal meaning, is a short function word, and its presence/absence correlates with past/future meaning); (c) which is the negation particle (it is constant, is a short function word, and its presence flips truth value); (d) the linear order among them. The key distributional evidence: the subject varies while tense and negation stay constant; tense and negation vary while the subject stays constant.

4. **Compose Layer 1 data: Simple intransitive sentences with tense variation.** Provide 3–4 sentences with intransitive verbs, a single pronoun subject, and varying tense (past, future, present/zero). Example pattern: [Subject] [Tense?] [Verb]. These sentences should allow the solver to induce: (a) the first element is the subject (it is a pronoun, it is semantically the agent); (b) the verb follows the subject; (c) an optional short word between subject and verb correlates with temporal meaning (past vs. future vs. absent). At this stage, the solver should be able to identify the subject, the verb, and the tense particle. The negation particle is NOT yet introduced, so the preverbal zone is simple (subject + optional tense).

5. **Compose Layer 2 data: Simple transitive sentences.** Provide 3–4 sentences with transitive verbs, a pronoun subject, and a noun or pronoun object. Example pattern: [Subject] [Tense?] [Verb] [Object]. These should allow the solver to induce: (a) the element after the verb is the object; (b) there is no morphological difference between a noun in subject position and the same noun in object position (no case marking); (c) word order is the sole cue for grammatical relations. Include at least one sentence where the same noun appears as subject in one sentence and as object in another, to make the absence of case marking visible. The solver should now be able to parse SVO structure reliably.

6. **Compose Layer 3 data: Negation.** Provide 3–4 sentences that include the negation particle. Include: (a) a negated intransitive sentence (Subject + Neg + Verb); (b) a negated transitive sentence (Subject + Neg + Verb + Object); (c) a negated sentence with past tense (Subject + Tense + Neg + Verb, or Subject + Neg + Tense + Verb — choose one order and be consistent); (d) a negated sentence with future tense. The solver should be able to induce: (a) a new preverbal element exists that is not the subject (it is constant, short, and flips truth value); (b) its linear position relative to the tense particle (this is a critical discovery — the solver must compare sentences with both tense and negation to determine their order); (c) negation is distinct from tense (different distribution: tense correlates with temporal meaning, negation correlates with truth-value reversal). This layer creates the full preverbal zone complexity.

7. **Compose Layer 4 data: Definite article.** Provide 3–4 sentences where a noun is preceded by "de" and 2–3 sentences where the same (or similar) noun is not preceded by "de". The contrast should correlate with definiteness: "de" appears when the referent is specific/known, absent when the referent is generic or indefinite. Example: "de fish" (the fish, a specific one) vs. "fish" (fish in general, or an unspecified fish). The solver should infer: (a) "de" is a prenominal element; (b) it is not a tense particle, negation, or subject (it appears in object position too, and it is not a pronoun); (c) it marks definiteness. This layer also helps the solver identify NP boundaries, which becomes important for Layer 5.

8. **Compose Layer 5 data: Postnominal relative clauses (basic).** Provide 3–4 sentences containing a relative clause following a noun. Use the gap strategy: the relativized element is simply absent from the relative clause. Include: (a) a relative clause modifying the object of a transitive verb, where the gap is the object (e.g., "the fish [that the child ate]" — the fish is missing from the RC); (b) a relative clause modifying the subject of a transitive verb, where the gap is the subject (e.g., "the child [that ate the fish]" — the child is missing from the RC); (c) a relative clause modifying the subject of an intransitive verb. The solver should induce: (a) a clause can follow a noun and modify it (postnominal RC); (b) the RC contains a verb and has its own arguments; (c) one argument is missing from the RC — this missing argument is the one that corresponds to the head noun's role (gap strategy). The solver must perform a "fill-in-the-blank" inference: identify the verb in the RC, determine its expected argument structure (from earlier layers), and identify which argument is absent.

9. **Compose Layer 6 data: Noun + Demonstrative (postnominal ambiguity).** Provide 2–3 sentences where a noun is followed by a demonstrative (a single word indicating spatial/deictic reference, e.g., 'this' or 'that'). These should be structurally similar to Layer 5 in that a word follows the noun, but the demonstrative is a single word with deictic meaning, not a clause with a verb. The solver should be able to distinguish: (a) N + Demonstrative (a single deictic word, no verb, no gap) from (b) N + Relative Clause (a clause with a verb, a gap, and potentially other arguments). This layer creates a genuine structural ambiguity that the solver must resolve. The definite article from Layer 4 helps here: "de N + Dem" vs. "de N + RC" — the article marks the left edge of the NP, and the solver must determine what follows.

10. **Compose Layer 7 data: Complex sentences combining multiple features.** Provide 2–3 sentences that combine: (a) a transitive main clause with tense and/or negation; (b) a relative clause modifying the subject or object; (c) the definite article on the head noun of the RC; (d) a gap in the RC that requires identifying the relativized role. These are the most complex sentences and require the solver to apply all induced rules simultaneously: parse the preverbal zone (subject + tense + negation), identify the main verb and object, recognize the postnominal RC, identify the gap within the RC, and determine the relativized role. Include at least one sentence where the RC's internal verb is transitive (creating a nested structure where the solver must identify both the main clause's arguments and the RC's arguments, with one of the RC's arguments being the gap).

11. **Verify the data set for unique inducibility.** Check the following: (a) SVO order is unambiguous — no sentence should allow a VSO or OVS reading given the evidence; (b) the preverbal zone is parseable — the subject, tense, and negation can be distinguished using distributional evidence (subject varies, function words are constant); (c) the relative order of tense and negation is determinable from at least one sentence containing both; (d) the definite article is not confusable with any other prenominal element (it never appears in a position where a subject or tense particle would be expected); (e) the gap in the RC is uniquely identifiable — the verb's argument structure (established in Layers 1–2) allows the solver to determine which argument is missing; (f) the demonstrative is not confusable with a relative clause (the demonstrative is a single word with no verb; the RC contains a verb); (g) no sentence is ambiguous in a way that would lead to an incorrect rule induction. Ensure each rule requires at least two data points for confirmation.

12. **Design Question 1 (Translation to English): Progressive difficulty.** Create 3 sub-questions of increasing complexity: (a) a simple sentence using only features from Layers 1–3 (e.g., a negated transitive sentence with past tense); (b) a sentence combining a transitive main clause with a postnominal relative clause and the definite article (Layers 4 + 5); (c) a complex sentence with a transitive main clause, tense, negation, a relative clause modifying the object with a gap, and a demonstrative on a different noun (Layers 3 + 4 + 5 + 6). The most complex question should require the solver to parse the preverbal zone, identify the RC, determine the gap, and distinguish a demonstrative from a relative clause.

13. **Design Question 2 (Translation to Ndyuka): Progressive difficulty.** Create 3–4 sub-questions: (a) a simple intransitive sentence with a specific tense; (b) a transitive sentence with negation and a specific tense; (c) a sentence with a relative clause modifying the subject, where the solver must produce the correct gap (omit the relativized element from the RC); (d) a sentence combining a definite article, a postnominal demonstrative, and a relative clause on different nouns, testing the solver's ability to produce both postnominal constructions correctly. Ensure each sub-question tests a combination of rules not directly present in the data set.

14. **Design Question 3 (Explanation).** Require the solver to explicitly state the rules they have induced: (a) basic word order; (b) the identity and position of tense particles; (c) the identity and position of the negation particle and its order relative to tense; (d) the definite article and its function; (e) the structure of postnominal relative clauses and the gap strategy; (f) the postnominal demonstrative and how it differs from a relative clause. This confirms that the solver has correctly identified all rules rather than merely pattern-matching.

15. **Final ambiguity check on questions.** For each question, verify that the intended answer is the unique correct answer given the induced rules. Pay special attention to: (a) relative clause attachment — if a sentence could be parsed with the RC attaching to a different noun, either accept multiple readings or revise the sentence; (b) gap identification — if the RC's verb could take different argument structures, ensure the data has established the correct one; (c) preverbal zone parsing — if a sentence has both tense and negation, ensure the order is unambiguous given the data. For the most complex question, it is acceptable to accept multiple readings as long as the solver's explanation shows they understand the source of the ambiguity.
