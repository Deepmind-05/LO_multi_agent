

TARGET-LANGUAGE: Sanuma (snm), Yanomami family, South America

CONSTRUCTION RATIONALE

1. **Establish the core parameter set and design constraints.** The puzzle language is Sanuma. Fix the following parameters before composing any data: (a) basic word order is SOV; (b) alignment is ergative-absolutive, with absolutive (S/O) unmarked and ergative (A) marked by a postpositional clitic; (c) case markers are postpositional clitics attached to or immediately following the noun (monoexponential); (d) the verb carries no person or number marking of any argument — verb forms are invariant with respect to argument identity (if any verbal morphology is included, it must encode only non-personal categories such as evidentiality, and must be positionally and morphologically distinct from case markers); (e) relative clauses are correlative or internally-headed, with a correlative element (pronoun or repeated noun) appearing inside the embedded clause that carries case marking revealing the head's grammatical role; (f) an antipassive construction exists in which the agent is absolutive and the patient is expressed as an oblique complement with a distinct oblique marker; (g) first-person plural pronouns distinguish inclusive and exclusive. These parameters are fixed design choices; the solver must discover them from the data.

2. **Design the case marking system.** Define three distinct markers: (a) an ergative postpositional clitic (call it -E) that follows the transitive agent; (b) zero marking for the absolutive (both intransitive subject and transitive object are unmarked); (c) an oblique postpositional clitic (call it -O) that marks the patient in the antipassive and any other oblique arguments. The three markers must be morphologically distinct from each other and from any other morphemes in the language. The ergative and oblique markers are postpositional (they follow the noun), which is a key contrast with the source language's prepositional system and must be evident from the data. The absolutive is the unmarked form, which is the central feature that makes the alignment ergative-absolutive rather than nominative-accusative.

3. **Design the pronoun system.** Create a set of independent pronouns that cover at least: 1SG, 2SG, 3SG, 1PL-inclusive, 1PL-exclusive, and optionally 2PL or 3PL. All pronouns carry the same case marking as full NPs: the ergative clitic -E follows the pronoun when it is a transitive agent, and the pronoun is unmarked when it is absolutive (S or O). The inclusive and exclusive 1PL pronouns must be distinct forms (different opaque strings). The 3SG pronoun should be distinct from the 1PL forms. The pronoun system must be consistent with the NP case-marking system — the same alignment pattern holds for both.

4. **Design the correlative relative clause system.** Specify that relative clauses are formed by placing a correlative element (a pronoun or a repeated form of the head noun) inside the embedded clause. The head noun appears outside (before) the embedded clause. The correlative element inside the clause carries case marking that reflects the head's grammatical role in the embedded event: if the head is the agent of the embedded clause, the correlative element is ergative-marked; if the head is the patient, it is unmarked (absolutive); if the head is the subject of an intransitive embedded clause, it is unmarked. The embedded clause follows the same SOV word order as main clauses. The correlative element is identifiable as a pronoun or repeated noun form, not as a free relative marker. No dedicated relative clause marker is used.

5. **Design the antipassive construction.** In the antipassive, the agent is expressed as an absolutive (unmarked) NP in subject position, and the patient is expressed as an oblique complement marked with -O. The word order is: Agent (unmarked) + Patient (-O) + Verb, consistent with the XOV pattern (oblique precedes object position, verb is final). The verb form in the antipassive is the same as in the transitive (no special verbal morphology is required for the antipassive, though the setter may optionally add a verbal marker if desired — if so, it must be clearly distinct from case markers). The antipassive is a valence-reducing operation: it demotes the patient from a core argument to an oblique.

6. **Compose Layer 1 data: Simple intransitive clauses.** Provide 2–3 sentences with intransitive verbs and a single unmarked subject NP (use 3rd-person full NPs, not pronouns, in this layer). The pattern is: NP + V. These sentences establish: (a) the verb is final (SOV order, at least SV for intransitives); (b) the intransitive subject is unmarked (absolutive); (c) the verb does not change form based on the subject's identity (no verbal person marking) — use different subjects with the same verb form to make this visible. The solver should be able to infer that the language has a final verb and that the single NP in an intransitive clause is unmarked. At this stage, the solver cannot yet distinguish S from A because no transitive clauses are present.

7. **Compose Layer 2 data: Simple transitive clauses.** Provide 2–3 sentences with transitive verbs, an ergative-marked agent, and an unmarked object. The pattern is: NP-E + NP + V. Use different nouns for agent and object across examples. These sentences, combined with Layer 1, allow the solver to infer: (a) the first NP in a transitive clause carries a postpositional marker (-E) that the intransitive subject does not carry; (b) the second NP is unmarked, just like the intransitive subject; (c) the alignment is ergative-absolutive (S and O share the unmarked form; A is marked); (d) the word order is SOV (agent, object, verb). The critical contrast is between Layer 1 (unmarked S) and Layer 2 (marked A, unmarked O): the same unmarked form appears on S and O, while A has a distinct marker. This is the core alignment inference. The solver should also note that the marker is postpositional (follows the noun), not prepositional.

8. **Compose Layer 3 data: Antipassive clauses.** Provide 1–2 sentences in the antipassive. The pattern is: NP (unmarked) + NP-O + V. The agent is unmarked (like an intransitive subject), and the patient carries the oblique marker -O, which is distinct from both the ergative -E and the zero absolutive. These sentences, combined with Layers 1 and 2, allow the solver to infer: (a) there is a third case marker (-O) distinct from -E and zero; (b) the agent in this construction is unmarked, just like an intransitive subject, even though a patient is present — this is not a simple intransitive; (c) the construction is a valence-reducing operation (antipassive) that demotes the patient to oblique; (d) the alignment system is confirmed: the same noun that is ergative in the transitive (Layer 2) is absolutive in the antipassive, showing that case marking tracks grammatical role, not semantic role. The solver should be able to distinguish the antipassive from a simple intransitive by the presence of the oblique-marked NP.

9. **Compose Layer 4 data: Correlative relative clauses.** Provide 2–3 sentences containing correlative relative clauses. Include at least: (a) a relative clause where the head is the agent of the embedded transitive clause (the correlative element inside is ergative-marked); (b) a relative clause where the head is the patient of the embedded transitive clause (the correlative element inside is unmarked/absolutive); (c) optionally, a relative clause where the head is the subject of an intransitive embedded clause (correlative element unmarked). The head noun appears before the embedded clause. The embedded clause follows SOV order. The correlative element (a pronoun or repeated noun form) appears in the appropriate argument position inside the embedded clause with the appropriate case marking. These sentences allow the solver to infer: (a) the structure is a head noun followed by an embedded clause (not a postnominal RC with a dedicated marker as in the source language); (b) the correlative element inside the clause is a pronoun or repeated noun, not a dedicated relative marker; (c) the case marking of the correlative element reveals the head's grammatical role in the embedded event; (d) the embedded clause follows the same word order and case-marking rules as main clauses. The solver must simultaneously parse the RC structure and interpret the case marking of the internal element.

10. **Compose Layer 5 data: Pronouns with inclusive/exclusive 1PL.** Provide 2–3 sentences using pronouns, including at least one 1PL-inclusive and one 1PL-exclusive in different grammatical roles. For example: (a) a transitive clause where 1PL-inclusive is the agent (ergative-marked) and 3SG is the object (unmarked); (b) a transitive clause where 1PL-exclusive is the agent (ergative-marked) and 3SG is the object; (c) optionally, an intransitive clause with 1PL-inclusive or 1PL-exclusive as the subject (unmarked). The referential context (provided through the English gloss or a brief situational note) must make it clear whether the addressee is included in the group. These sentences allow the solver to infer: (a) there are two distinct 1PL pronoun forms; (b) one is inclusive (addressee included) and the other is exclusive (addressee excluded); (c) pronouns carry the same case marking as full NPs (ergative -E on transitive agents, unmarked on absolutive); (d) the alignment system holds in the pronominal domain. The two-dimensional identification task (person/number including inclusive/exclusive AND grammatical role) is the key challenge here: the solver must simultaneously identify which 1PL form is which AND what case it is in.

11. **Compose Layer 6 data: Complex combinations.** Provide 2–3 sentences that combine multiple features simultaneously. Include at least: (a) a transitive main clause with a correlative relative clause modifying the agent (the head is ergative in the main clause, and the correlative element inside the RC is also ergative if the head is the agent of the embedded clause, or unmarked if the head is the patient); (b) a sentence with an antipassive main clause and a relative clause; (c) optionally, a sentence with a 1PL inclusive/exclusive pronoun as the agent of a transitive clause that also contains a relative clause. These sentences require the solver to apply all previously induced rules simultaneously: word order, case marking, RC parsing, antipassive identification, and pronoun identification. They serve as the highest-difficulty items and test whether the solver has truly internalized the system rather than pattern-matching on individual features.

12. **Verify the data set for unique inducibility and consistency.** Check the following: (a) the ergative-absolutive alignment is unambiguously identifiable — there must be at least one intransitive clause showing an unmarked S and at least one transitive clause showing a marked A and unmarked O, so the solver cannot confuse this with a nominative-accusative system; (b) the postpositional nature of case markers is clear — the marker always follows the noun, never precedes it; (c) the antipassive is distinguishable from a simple intransitive — the oblique-marked patient must be present and the oblique marker must be distinct from both ergative and absolutive; (d) the correlative RC structure is unambiguous — the head noun is clearly outside the embedded clause, and the correlative element inside is clearly a pronoun or repeated noun (not a dedicated marker); (e) the inclusive/exclusive distinction is inferable from context — at least one example of each type must be provided with clear referential context; (f) no verbal person marking is present — verb forms must not vary by person or number of arguments; (g) all case markers are consistent across all examples with no irregular or optional marking; (h) no rule can be inferred from a single sentence alone — each rule requires at least two data points for confirmation.

13. **Design Question 1: Translation from Sanuma to English (progressive difficulty).** Create 3 sub-questions of increasing complexity: (a) a simple transitive sentence with a correlative relative clause modifying the object — tests basic alignment, word order, and RC parsing; (b) an antipassive sentence with a relative clause modifying the agent — tests antipassive identification, oblique marking, and RC parsing simultaneously; (c) a complex sentence with a 1PL inclusive/exclusive pronoun as the agent, a correlative relative clause modifying the object, and the embedded clause being transitive — tests all systems simultaneously. Each sub-question should require the solver to apply rules not directly present in the training data in the exact same combination.

14. **Design Question 2: Translation from English to Sanuma (progressive difficulty).** Create 3–4 sub-questions: (a) a simple intransitive sentence with a 3rd-person subject — tests basic SOV and unmarked absolutive; (b) a transitive sentence with a 1PL-exclusive agent and a correlative relative clause modifying the object where the head is the patient of the embedded clause — tests ergative marking, inclusive/exclusive identification, and RC case marking; (c) an antipassive sentence with a relative clause modifying the oblique patient — tests antipassive construction and RC structure; (d) optionally, a sentence requiring the solver to choose between inclusive and exclusive 1PL based on the referential context given in the English source. Each sub-question should test a different combination of rules.

15. **Design Question 3: Explanation of induced rules.** Require the solver to explicitly state: (a) the basic word order; (b) the case-marking system (what is marked, what is unmarked, where the markers appear); (c) the alignment type and how it was determined; (d) the structure of relative clauses and how the head's role is indicated; (e) the antipassive construction and how it differs from the basic transitive; (f) the inclusive/exclusive distinction and how it is identified. This question confirms that the solver has correctly identified all rules rather than merely pattern-matching, and forces explicit articulation of the reasoning.

16. **Final ambiguity check on questions.** For each question, verify that the intended answer is the unique correct answer given the induced rules. Specifically check: (a) no relative clause attachment ambiguity (if a sentence could be parsed with the RC modifying a different NP, either accept multiple readings or revise the sentence); (b) no confusion between antipassive and intransitive (the oblique-marked patient must be present in antipassive examples); (c) no confusion between inclusive and exclusive 1PL (the referential context must be unambiguous); (d) the correlative element inside the RC is not confused with a main-clause argument (its position and case marking should make its role clear). If a question admits multiple valid readings, either accept multiple answers explicitly or revise to eliminate the ambiguity.
