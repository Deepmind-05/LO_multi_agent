

TARGET-LANGUAGE: Evenki (Tungusic, Altaic)

CONSTRUCTION RATIONALE

1. **Define the grammatical parameter set.** Establish the following as the target-language profile: (a) SOV basic constituent order; (b) nominative-accusative alignment with zero nominative and a distinct accusative suffix on the transitive object; (c) a rich suffixal case system (10+ cases, no syncretism, symmetrical marking); (d) a suffixal verbal complex encoding tense, aspect, evidentiality, and (in negative sentences) a negative suffix, plus person agreement; (e) obligatory double negation consisting of a preverbal negative word and a postverbal negative suffix, neither of which occurs without the other; (f) prenominal relative clauses using a gap strategy (no relative pronoun or resumptive element); (g) no articles, no dedicated existential verb, no prefixal morphology. These parameters define the full space of properties the solver must discover.

2. **Choose a controlled vocabulary set.** Select 10–14 content words spanning: (a) 3–4 intransitive verbs (e.g., 'arrive', 'sleep', 'fall', 'run'); (b) 3–4 transitive verbs (e.g., 'see', 'give', 'build', 'eat'); (c) 5–6 common nouns (e.g., 'dog', 'river', 'house', 'child', 'fish', 'mountain'); (d) 2–3 proper names or 3rd-person pronouns; (e) 1–2 adverbs (e.g., 'always', 'also'). Ensure all nouns are countable and that semantic roles (agent, patient, location, recipient) are unambiguous in the intended translations. The vocabulary should be small enough that the solver can track all forms across the data set but large enough to prevent accidental pattern-matching.

3. **Design the case marking system.** Define the following case markers as suffixes on nouns: (a) nominative = zero (unmarked); (b) accusative = a distinct suffix (call it C_acc) marking the transitive object; (c) locative = a distinct suffix (C_loc) marking location; (d) dative = a distinct suffix (C_dat) marking recipient/beneficiary. Optionally include one or two more cases (e.g., ablative, comitative) if the puzzle benefits from showing greater richness. All case suffixes must be morphologically distinct from each other and from verbal suffixes. The nominative-accusative alignment is the core pattern: S and A are both zero-marked, P takes C_acc. The additional cases (locative, dative) are not alignment-relevant but demonstrate the richness of the system. The solver must distinguish alignment-relevant marking from other case marking.

4. **Design the verbal suffixal complex.** Define the verb as a stem followed by a suffixal string. The suffixal complex should encode, in a fixed order: (a) aspect (perfective vs. imperfective); (b) tense (past vs. non-past); (c) evidentiality (direct vs. indirect); (d) negative suffix (only in negative sentences); (e) person/number agreement. Each element should be a distinct suffix. The person/number suffix should mark the subject (S or A) and, in transitive clauses, also the object (P) — reflecting the accusative alignment in verbal morphology (only A is marked on the verb in the sense that the verb agrees with A for person, while P is marked by the accusative case on the NP). The key design principle: the verbal complex is a single morphological string that the solver must segment into functional units. This is the central morphological analysis challenge of the puzzle.

5. **Design the negation system.** Specify that negation requires two elements in fixed positions: (a) a preverbal negative word (call it NegW) that appears immediately before the verb (i.e., between the object and the verb in SOV order); (b) a postverbal negative suffix (NegS) that is part of the verbal suffixal complex. Neither element occurs without the other. In positive sentences, neither element is present. The preverbal word is a separate lexical item (not a clitic or affix). This creates a clear, observable pattern: negative sentences have an extra word before the verb AND an extra suffix on the verb, while positive sentences have neither. The solver must identify both elements, their fixed positions, and their obligatory co-occurrence.

6. **Design the relative clause system.** Specify that relative clauses are prenominal (they precede the head noun) and use a gap strategy: the relativized element is simply absent from the relative clause, with no relative pronoun, resumptive pronoun, or dedicated marker. The relative clause is a finite clause (it carries its own tense/aspect marking on the verb). The case on the head noun (or its absence) helps the solver identify which argument is gapped: if the head noun is in nominative (zero), the gap is the subject; if in accusative, the gap is the object; if in locative, the gap is a locative argument. The relative clause follows the same case-marking rules as independent clauses for its remaining arguments.

7. **Compose the data set — Layer 1: Simple intransitive sentences.** Provide 3–4 sentences with intransitive verbs, a single zero-marked subject, and varying tense/aspect values. These sentences should allow the solver to induce: (a) SOV order (the verb consistently appears at the end of the clause, the subject precedes it); (b) the subject is unmarked (zero/nominative); (c) the verb carries a suffixal complex encoding at least tense and aspect (the solver should notice that the verb form changes with tense/aspect, and that the change is suffixal, not prefixal). At this stage, the solver should NOT yet be able to fully segment the verbal complex — they should notice that the verb has a variable suffixal portion but may not yet identify all its components. This is intentional: full segmentation will come later.

8. **Compose the data set — Layer 2: Simple transitive sentences.** Provide 3–4 sentences with transitive verbs, a zero-marked agent, and an accusative-marked object. These sentences should allow the solver to induce: (a) the transitive object takes a distinct suffix (C_acc) that was not seen on the intransitive subject; (b) the transitive agent is zero-marked, identical to the intransitive subject — this is the critical contrast that establishes nominative-accusative alignment (S = A = zero, P = C_acc); (c) the word order remains SOV (subject, object, verb). The solver should now be able to confirm that the alignment is NOT ergative-absolutive (where S and P would share marking and A would be distinct). The contrast between Layer 1 (S = zero) and Layer 2 (A = zero, P = C_acc) is the key evidence.

9. **Compose the data set — Layer 3: Sentences with additional case markers.** Provide 2–3 sentences that include a locative or dative argument (e.g., "The child arrived at the river" with the river in locative; "The dog gave the fish to the child" with the child in dative). These sentences should allow the solver to induce: (a) the case system is richer than just nominative-accusative; (b) additional case suffixes mark semantic roles beyond agent and patient; (c) these additional cases are distinct from the accusative and from each other. This layer extends the solver's understanding of the case system and prepares them to interpret case on head nouns in relative clauses (Layer 6).

10. **Compose the data set — Layer 4: Negative sentences.** Provide 3–4 negative sentences (mix of intransitive and transitive). Each negative sentence should contain both the preverbal negative word (NegW) and the postverbal negative suffix (NegS). The positive counterparts from Layers 1–3 should make it clear that neither element is present in positive sentences. These sentences should allow the solver to induce: (a) negation requires two elements; (b) one element is a separate word positioned immediately before the verb (between object and verb in SOV order); (c) the other element is a suffix on the verb, part of the verbal suffixal complex; (d) the two elements are obligatorily co-occurring (neither appears alone). The solver should also begin to notice that the verbal suffixal complex in negative sentences is longer than in positive sentences, hinting at the additional suffix.

11. **Compose the data set — Layer 5: Evidentiality minimal pairs.** Provide 2–3 pairs of sentences that are identical in all respects except for evidentiality: one sentence describes a directly witnessed event, the other describes an indirectly known event (e.g., "I saw the dog run" vs. "I heard that the dog ran"). The tense, aspect, person, and all other elements should be held constant. The only difference should be a suffix on the verb. These sentences should allow the solver to induce: (a) the verbal suffixal complex encodes a dimension of meaning beyond tense and aspect; (b) this dimension distinguishes direct from indirect evidence; (c) it occupies a specific position in the suffixal string. This layer is critical for full segmentation of the verbal complex: by now, the solver has seen tense/aspect variation (Layer 1), negative suffix (Layer 4), and now evidentiality. The solver should be able to begin identifying the ordering of suffixes in the verbal complex.

12. **Compose the data set — Layer 6: Relative clauses.** Provide 3–4 sentences containing prenominal relative clauses. Include: (a) a relative clause modifying the object of a transitive verb (the gap is the object, identifiable because the head noun is in accusative); (b) a relative clause modifying the subject of a transitive verb (the gap is the subject, identifiable because the head noun is in nominative/zero); (c) optionally, a relative clause modifying a locative argument (the gap is the location, identifiable because the head noun is in locative). For at least one example, provide the corresponding independent clause as a separate sentence so the solver can compare and identify the gap. These sentences should allow the solver to induce: (a) the relative clause precedes the head noun (prenominal); (b) the relativized element is absent from the relative clause (gap strategy — no relative pronoun or marker); (c) the case on the head noun identifies which argument is gapped; (d) the remaining arguments inside the relative clause follow the same case-marking rules as in independent clauses.

13. **Compose the data set — Layer 7: Complex sentences combining multiple features.** Provide 2–3 sentences that combine: (a) a transitive main clause with accusative object and zero agent; (b) a prenominal relative clause modifying one of the arguments; (c) a specific tense/aspect value; (d) evidentiality; (e) optionally, negation (testing the interaction of double negation with the full verbal complex). These sentences should be the most complex in the data set and should require the solver to apply all induced rules simultaneously: parse the SOV structure, identify the case on each NP, segment the verbal suffixal complex into its components (aspect, tense, evidentiality, negative suffix, person), identify the preverbal negative word if present, and parse the prenominal relative clause with its gap. At least one sentence should have a relative clause whose internal verb is itself transitive (creating a nested structure with its own accusative marking).

14. **Verify the data set for unique inducibility.** Check the following: (a) SOV order is unambiguous — the verb is always last, and no sentence could be misread as SVO or VSO; (b) nominative-accusative alignment is clearly established — there is at least one intransitive sentence showing zero on S, and at least one transitive sentence showing zero on A and C_acc on P, making the S=A≠P pattern inescapable; (c) the verbal suffixal complex is segmentable — the solver has enough variation across sentences to identify each suffix's position and function (tense varies across sentences, aspect varies, evidentiality varies in minimal pairs, negative suffix appears only in negative sentences); (d) double negation is clearly obligatory — at least 2 negative examples show both elements, and all positive examples show neither; (e) the prenominal RC position is unambiguous — the modifying clause always precedes the head noun; (f) the gap strategy is identifiable — at least one RC has a corresponding independent clause for comparison; (g) no rule can be inferred from a single sentence alone — each rule requires at least two data points; (h) no sentence is ambiguous in a way that would lead to an incorrect rule induction.

15. **Design Question 1 (Translation to English): Progressive difficulty.** Create 3 sub-questions: (a) a simple intransitive sentence with a specific tense/aspect and evidentiality value (tests basic SOV parsing and verbal complex segmentation); (b) a transitive sentence with a locative or dative argument and a specific evidentiality value (tests case identification and verbal complex segmentation); (c) a complex sentence with a prenominal relative clause (modifying the object), a specific tense/aspect, evidentiality, and negation (tests all features simultaneously: SOV parsing, case identification, verbal complex segmentation including negative suffix, preverbal negative word identification, RC parsing with gap).

16. **Design Question 2 (Translation to target language): Progressive difficulty.** Create 3–4 sub-questions: (a) a simple intransitive sentence with a specific tense, aspect, and evidentiality value (tests production of the correct verbal suffixal complex); (b) a transitive sentence with a dative argument and a specific evidentiality value (tests correct case selection and verbal complex production); (c) a negative transitive sentence with a specific tense and evidentiality value (tests production of both negation elements in correct positions and the full verbal complex); (d) a sentence with a prenominal relative clause modifying the subject, with a specific tense and evidentiality value in both the main clause and the relative clause (tests RC production with gap strategy and correct case on the head noun). Ensure each sub-question tests a combination of rules not directly present as an identical surface form in the data set.

17. **Design Question 3 (Explanation).** Require the solver to explicitly state the rules they have induced: (a) basic word order; (b) the alignment system (which arguments share marking, which are distinct); (c) the case system (list the case suffixes and their functions); (d) the structure of the verbal suffixal complex (list the suffixes in order and their functions); (e) the negation system (identify both elements, their positions, and their obligatory co-occurrence); (f) the relative clause strategy (position and gap). This confirms that the solver has correctly identified all rules rather than merely pattern-matching.

18. **Final ambiguity check on questions.** For each question, verify that the intended answer is the unique correct answer given the induced rules. Specific checks: (a) in Question 1(c), verify that the relative clause attachment is unambiguous (the head noun's case should make it clear which argument is relativized); (b) in Question 2(c), verify that the solver can determine the correct order of suffixes in the verbal complex from the data (the ordering should be consistent across all data sentences); (c) in Question 2(d), verify that the gap in the relative clause is unambiguous given the head noun's case; (d) if any question admits multiple valid translations due to genuine ambiguity in the data, either accept multiple answers explicitly or revise the sentence to eliminate the ambiguity.
