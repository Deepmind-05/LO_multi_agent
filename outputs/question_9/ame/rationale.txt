

TARGET-LANGUAGE: Amele (Trans-New Guinea / Madang / Mabuso)

CONSTRUCTION RATIONALE

1. **Establish the parsing framework through postnominal modification.** Before introducing any classification or agreement data, present a small set of noun phrases in which adjectives, demonstratives, and numerals consistently follow the head noun. The purpose of this step is to give the solver a reliable segmentation tool: they should be able to identify the head noun in any noun phrase by recognizing that modifiers come after it. This is a low-difficulty discovery that serves as infrastructure for all subsequent steps. The solver should infer: "In this language, the noun is the head and modifiers follow it." Do not yet introduce possessive suffixes or plural forms in this initial set—keep the noun phrases simple (noun + one or two postnominal modifiers) so the pattern is unambiguous. This step also implicitly establishes that the language is suffixing: the noun itself carries no overt prefixal or infixed marking in these examples.

2. **Introduce possessive data to elicit discovery of the possessive classification system.** Present a set of noun + possessive suffix pairs (e.g., "my X," "your X," "his/her X" for various nouns). Design the data so that nouns fall into at least 5 distinct classes based on which possessive suffixes they take. Key design constraints: (a) Each class must be represented by at least 3–4 nouns to allow reliable induction; (b) The possessive suffixes should be morphologically distinct across classes (different suffixes for the same possessor), so the solver can group nouns by their suffix set; (c) The classes should NOT correlate with semantic categories (e.g., animate/inanimate, body parts/tools) in any obvious way—this forces the solver to treat the classification as morphological rather than semantic; (d) Include at least one pair of nouns that are semantically similar but belong to different possessive classes, to prevent the solver from hypothesizing a semantic basis. The solver should infer: "Nouns are grouped into classes based on which possessive suffixes they take; these classes are not determined by meaning." This is the first major classification system the solver encounters.

3. **Introduce nominal plural data to elicit discovery of the human-only plural restriction.** Present a set of nouns, some of which have two forms (singular and plural) and some of which have only one form. The nouns with two forms should all be human referents (people, kinship terms, etc.), and the nouns with one form should all be non-human. Design constraints: (a) Include at least 4–5 human nouns with plural forms and at least 5–6 non-human nouns without plural forms, so the pattern is statistically clear; (b) The plural forms should use at least two different morphological strategies (e.g., different suffixes, or a suffix plus reduplication for some nouns) to realize the "mixed plural strategies" concept; (c) The plural strategies should NOT correlate with possessive class—a noun in possessive class A might use plural strategy 1, while another noun in possessive class A uses plural strategy 2. This is critical: it forces the solver to recognize that possessive class and plural strategy are independent systems. The solver should infer: "Only human nouns have plural forms; the plural is marked by different strategies depending on the noun; this is independent of possessive class." This step also implicitly teaches the solver that "number" in this language is a restricted category, not a universal one.

4. **Introduce intransitive verb data to begin building the agreement paradigm.** Present a set of intransitive clauses (one argument, the subject). The verb should carry a person/number marker that agrees with the subject. Design constraints: (a) Include subjects that are 1st, 2nd, and 3rd person, singular and plural; (b) The 3rd person singular and 3rd person plural forms should be distinct (this is the seed of the cross-paradigm link, though the solver does not yet know why); (c) Introduce syncretism at this stage: at least one pair of person/number combinations should share the same verbal form (e.g., 1st person singular and 1st person plural are syncretic, or 2nd person singular and 2nd person plural are syncretic). The solver should infer: "The verb marks the subject's person and number; not all combinations have distinct forms." The syncretism prevents the solver from assuming a fully regular paradigm and forces them to track which forms are shared. This is the first encounter with the verb system.

5. **Introduce transitive verb data to elicit discovery of the P-before-A order and the full agreement paradigm.** Present a set of transitive clauses (two arguments: A and P). The verb should carry two agreement markers: one for P (object) and one for A (subject). Design constraints: (a) The P marker must consistently precede the A marker in all transitive examples—this is a fixed morphological order; (b) Include enough transitive examples to cover the major person/number combinations for both A and P; (c) The syncretism from step 4 should be visible here as well: when the P marker is syncretic with another form, the solver must use the structural position (first marker = P, second = A) to disambiguate; (d) Include at least one transitive example where both A and P are 3rd person, so the solver sees the 3sg-3sg and 3pl-3pl combinations. The solver should infer: "In transitive clauses, the verb marks both arguments; the object marker comes before the subject marker; syncretic forms are disambiguated by position." This step builds on step 4 (the intransitive paradigm) and extends it to the transitive case.

6. **Create the cross-paradigm link as the central "aha" moment.** This is the critical inference that connects the nominal and verbal systems. The data from steps 3–5 should be structured so that the solver can now notice: when a human noun appears in its plural form as an argument, the verb takes a plural agreement marker; when a human noun appears in its singular form, the verb takes a singular agreement marker; when a non-human noun appears (which has no plural form), the verb always takes a singular agreement marker regardless of semantic number. Design constraints: (a) Include at least 2–3 examples where a plural human noun is the subject and the verb shows plural agreement; (b) Include at least 2–3 examples where a singular human noun is the subject and the verb shows singular agreement; (c) Include at least 2–3 examples where a non-human noun (semantically plural, e.g., "the stones," "the birds") is the subject and the verb shows SINGULAR agreement—this is the crucial contrast that reveals the restriction; (d) The same pattern should hold for the P (object) position in transitive clauses. The solver should infer: "Verbal number agreement is not based on semantic number but on whether the nominal argument is a human noun in its plural form. Non-human nouns always trigger singular agreement." This is the central discovery that unifies the nominal and verbal systems.

7. **Introduce the adjective/relative clause collapse as a layer of productive ambiguity.** Present a set of noun phrases in which postnominal modifiers are ambiguous between adjectival and relative-clausal interpretation. For example, a form like "X [modifier]" could mean "X that is [adjective]" or "X who/which [relative clause]." Design constraints: (a) The ambiguous forms should be structurally identical to clear adjectives and clear relative clauses in other examples—the only difference is interpretive; (b) Include at least 2–3 examples where context makes the interpretation clear (e.g., the modifier contains a subject marker, making it clearly a relative clause; or the modifier is a simple quality word, making it clearly an adjective); (c) Include at least 2–3 examples where the interpretation is genuinely ambiguous from the form alone. The solver should infer: "This language does not formally distinguish adjectives from relative clauses; both are postnominal and share the same formal properties; some instances are ambiguous." This adds a layer of uncertainty to noun phrase analysis that the solver must acknowledge rather than resolve. It interacts with step 1 (postnominal modification) by showing that while the solver can identify WHERE modifiers go, they cannot always determine WHAT TYPE of modifier is present.

8. **Design the question set to test progressive understanding.** Structure the questions so that each builds on the previous discoveries:
   - **Q1 (Parsing and classification):** Present a set of noun phrases (some possessive, some with postnominal modifiers, some with plural forms). Ask the solver to (a) identify the head noun in each phrase, (b) assign each noun to its possessive class, (c) identify which nouns are human (have plural forms) and which are non-human. This tests steps 1–3.
   - **Q2 (Verb paradigm completion):** Present a set of verb forms with some slots missing (intransitive and transitive). Ask the solver to fill in the missing agreement markers. Include at least one case where syncretism means the solver must use structural position to determine which marker is which. This tests steps 4–5.
   - **Q3 (Cross-paradigm prediction):** Present a set of sentences with the nominal argument given (in singular or plural form, human or non-human) and the verb form missing. Ask the solver to predict the correct verbal agreement. Include at least one case where the nominal argument is a non-human noun that is semantically plural (e.g., "the many stones") to test whether the solver has truly internalized the human-only restriction. This tests step 6.
   - **Q4 (Ambiguity recognition):** Present a set of noun phrases with postnominal modifiers. Ask the solver to identify which are unambiguously adjectival, which are unambiguously relative-clausal, and which are ambiguous. Ask the solver to explain why the ambiguous ones cannot be resolved from form alone. This tests step 7.
   - **Q5 (Explanation):** Ask the solver to provide a coherent account of the system: describe the possessive classification, the human-only plural restriction, the verb agreement paradigm (including syncretism and P-before-A order), and the cross-paradigm link between nominal number and verbal number. This ensures the solver has a theoretical understanding rather than pattern-matching.

9. **Verify solvability and check for unintended ambiguities.** Before finalizing the puzzle, verify: (a) Every possessive class is uniquely identifiable from the data (no two classes produce identical suffix sets for any possessor); (b) The human/non-human distinction is unambiguous from the nominal data (no non-human noun has a plural form, no human noun lacks one); (c) The verb paradigm is fully determinable from the data given the syncretism pattern (the solver can identify which forms are shared and which are distinct); (d) The cross-paradigm link is discoverable: the contrast between "plural human → plural verb" and "semantically plural non-human → singular verb" is present in the data and is the only explanation that accounts for all verbal number forms; (e) The adjective/relative clause ambiguity is genuine (the ambiguous forms are not resolvable by any formal cue in the data); (f) No question can be answered by simple analogy to a single training example without applying the abstract rule (i.e., the test items should involve nouns or person/number combinations not seen in the training data); (g) The total number of interacting systems (possessive class, plural strategy, verb agreement, syncretism, cross-paradigm link, modifier ambiguity) creates sufficient complexity for a challenging puzzle while remaining solvable from the evidence provided.

10. **Consider optional additions for additional depth.** If the puzzle needs additional complexity or the setter wants to incorporate optional concepts: (a) Optional double negation: include both single-negation (preverbal word only, or suffix only) and double-negation (both) forms in the data, so the solver can discover that the combination is optional. This adds a layer to verb analysis without disrupting the core architecture. (b) No second-person imperatives: if imperative forms are included, show 1st and 3rd person imperatives but leave 2nd person absent, creating a noticeable gap that tests whether the solver is building a complete paradigm. (c) Split nominal/locational predication: include a few "is" sentences where the predicate is nominal (zero copula or a specific marker) versus locational (a different marker or structure), so the solver discovers that predication type affects encoding. These optional elements should be introduced AFTER the core discoveries (steps 1–7) are established, so they do not interfere with the main inference chain.
