

TARGET-LANGUAGE: Modern Greek (grk)

CONSTRUCTION RATIONALE

1. **Establish the singular data set.** Present a table of 8–12 nouns, each with its singular definite article and a translation. The nouns must include at least 2–3 from each of the three gender classes (masculine, feminine, neuter). The singular article forms (ο, η, το) are the primary diagnostic for class membership. Select nouns whose semantic content is varied and does not correlate with gender (e.g., include neuter nouns for inanimate objects, feminine nouns for abstract concepts, masculine nouns for both animate and inanimate referents). This prevents the solver from using semantic shortcuts to assign class. The solver's first inference should be: "There are three groups of nouns, distinguished by their article form." This is the simplest discovery and provides the foundation for everything that follows.

2. **Introduce the plural data set.** Present the same nouns (or a subset) in the plural, with their plural definite articles and plural noun forms. The plural articles are οι (for both masculine and feminine) and τα (for neuter). The plural noun forms exhibit vowel alternations: neuter nouns ending in -ο show -α in the plural (e.g., βιβλίο → βιβλία); feminine nouns ending in -α show -ες with an internal ο (e.g., εικόνα → εικόνες); masculine nouns ending in -ας show -ες (e.g., άντρας → άντρες). The solver should now be able to observe: (a) the plural article distinguishes only two groups (neuter vs. non-neuter), not three; (b) the noun forms themselves show systematic vowel changes. The critical inference at this stage is the discovery of the syncretism: the article that was a perfect three-way diagnostic in the singular becomes a two-way diagnostic in the plural. The solver must now rely on the noun form to distinguish masculine from feminine plurals.

3. **Ensure the vowel alternations are class-conditioned and visible.** Within the data, the vowel changes must be consistent within each class but different across classes. The solver should be able to formulate: "Neuter nouns change their final vowel from ο to α; feminine nouns ending in α develop an internal ο and take -ες; masculine nouns ending in -ας drop the α and take -ες." The key design principle: the alternation is not a single general rule but a set of class-specific rules. The solver must discover that the class determines *which* alternation applies. This is the core of the vowel quality alternation concept.

4. **Introduce the final-vowel conditioning within classes.** Include at least one pair of nouns within the same class that have different final singular vowels and therefore different plural patterns. For example: neuter nouns ending in -ο (βιβλίο → βιβλία) vs. neuter nouns ending in -ι (παιδί → παιδιά); feminine nouns ending in -α (εικόνα → εικόνες) vs. feminine nouns ending in -η (γλώσσα → γλώσσες, or a noun like πόλη → πόλεις if using the -η → -εις pattern). This forces the solver to discover that class alone is insufficient to predict the exact plural form; the final singular vowel provides a second conditioning dimension. The solver's rule must be refined from "class → alternation" to "class + final vowel → specific alternation." This is the key difficulty escalation: the system is two-dimensional, not one-dimensional.

5. **Design the article as a partial cross-paradigm diagnostic.** The article paradigm (6 forms: ο/η/το in singular, οι/οι/τα in plural) should be presented as a separate small table or integrated into the noun data. The solver should discover that the article form encodes class + number, but that the encoding is not fully transparent in the plural due to syncretism. This creates the central inference tension: the article is a useful tool that *almost* works but fails in one specific way. The solver must identify exactly where it fails and what additional evidence is needed to compensate. This is the adapted cross-paradigm prediction concept: the article predicts the noun's class, but only partially.

6. **Design the "class identification" question.** Present 5–7 noun forms (a mix of singular and plural, some with articles, some without) and ask the solver to assign each to a class. Include at least one plural form where the article is οι (ambiguous between masculine and feminine) and the solver must use the noun's own form (suffix, vowel pattern) to resolve the ambiguity. Include at least one singular form where the article clearly identifies the class. This question tests whether the solver has internalized the class system and can use both the article and the noun form as diagnostics.

7. **Design the "plural prediction" question.** Present 6–8 nouns with only the singular form (and article) given, and ask the solver to produce the plural form (and plural article). Distribute the items so that: (a) at least 2–3 items have a unique, unambiguous prediction (the class and final vowel together determine a single plural form); (b) at least 1–2 items require the solver to apply a vowel alternation to a root not seen in the training data (testing generalization of the alternation rule); (c) at least 1 item is a neuter noun where the solver must produce the τα article (confirming the solver knows the neuter plural article differs from the non-neuter). The items should use different roots from the training data to ensure the solver is applying rules, not memorizing.

8. **Design the "article completion" question.** Present 4–5 noun forms (singular or plural) with the article missing, and ask the solver to supply the correct article. Include at least one plural form where the noun is masculine or feminine (both take οι) to confirm the solver understands the syncretism. Include at least one singular form to confirm the solver can use the noun's class to select the correct singular article. This question tests the cross-paradigm relationship in the reverse direction: given the noun, predict the article.

9. **Design the "explanation" question.** Ask the solver to: (a) describe the class system and how it is identified; (b) state the vowel alternation rules for each class; (c) explain why the plural article does not fully distinguish all classes and what additional evidence is needed; (d) explain how the final singular vowel affects the plural form within a class. This ensures the solver has a coherent theoretical account that integrates all discovered concepts.

10. **Verify solvability and absence of unintended shortcuts.** Check that: (a) no two classes produce identical singular-plural pairs for any root (each class is uniquely identifiable); (b) the semantic content of the nouns does not correlate with class (no semantic shortcut); (c) the article syncretism is clearly visible in the data (at least one masculine plural and one feminine plural are present so the solver can observe they share the article but differ in noun form); (d) the final-vowel conditioning is discoverable (at least one pair within a class shows different plural patterns due to different final vowels); (e) the total number of distinct features the solver must track (class, final vowel, article form, vowel alternation) is sufficient for a challenging puzzle but not so many that induction becomes impossible from the given data; (f) no verb forms appear anywhere in the puzzle (Modern Greek verbs do not agree in gender, and including them would create a false parallel or confusion).
