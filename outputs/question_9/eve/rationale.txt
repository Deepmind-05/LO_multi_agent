

TARGET-LANGUAGE

Evenki (Tungusic, Altaic)

CONSTRUCTION RATIONALE

1. **Establish the phonological and morphological framework.** Define the vowel inventory with a binary length contrast (short vs. long). Define the consonant inventory to be moderate in size (no need for exotic consonants). Establish that the language is agglutinative and suffixing: all derivational and inflectional morphology is realized by concatenating suffixes to a stem in a fixed order. For nouns, the order is: stem → plural suffix → case suffix. For verbs, the order is: stem → aspect/tense → evidential (if included) → person/number agreement. This ordering is the structural "skeleton" of the entire puzzle. The solver must discover it, not be told it. The vowel length contrast must be phonemic: there must be at least one minimal pair or a consistent morphological pattern where length is the only difference. Do not introduce tone. Do not introduce a noun class or gender system.

2. **Design the plural suffix and its vowel alternation.** The plural suffix is obligatory on all nouns in the plural. It has a surface alternation in its vowel (e.g., -la vs. -le). The conditioning factor for this alternation should be the vowel length of the stem-final vowel: stems ending in a long vowel take one variant (e.g., -le), stems ending in a short vowel take the other (e.g., -la). This creates a direct interaction between the vowel length concept (CORE) and the vowel quality alternation concept (CORE/OPTIONAL). The solver must discover both the existence of the alternation and its conditioning factor. Ensure that the alternation is regular and applies across all stems without exception. Provide at least 4–5 stems ending in long vowels and 4–5 stems ending in short vowels so the pattern is inductively recoverable. The consonant of the suffix should be invariant (no consonant alternation in the plural suffix itself), keeping the focus on the vowel.

3. **Design the case system for nouns.** Select 4–6 case suffixes (e.g., nominative/zero, accusative, locative, ablative, dative, instrumental). Each case suffix is a distinct, invariant string. The critical structural property is that case suffixes always follow the plural suffix. This means: singular nouns show stem + case; plural nouns show stem + plural + case. The solver must discover this ordering from the data. To make the ordering discoverable, ensure that at least one case suffix begins with a consonant that could be confused with the final consonant of the plural suffix if the solver mis-segments. This creates a segmentation challenge that the solver resolves by recognizing the fixed ordering. Do not include syncretism (no two cases share the same suffix). The number of cases should be enough to provide rich data (4–6) but not so many that the puzzle becomes unwieldy. If the puzzle is already complex enough from the verb side, 4 cases suffice.

4. **Design the noun data table.** Present a table of 12–18 nouns, each shown in at least two cases (one of which should be the "bare" or nominative form). Ensure the following distribution: (a) At least 6 nouns are shown in the plural (with the plural suffix visible); (b) The stems cover a range of final vowels, including both long and short, so the plural suffix alternation is visible; (c) At least 3 nouns are shown in the same case in both singular and plural, creating a direct contrast that isolates the plural suffix; (d) At least 2 nouns are shown in multiple cases in the plural, so the solver can see that the case suffix attaches after the plural suffix regardless of which case is involved; (e) Include at least 2 stems where the final consonant is the same as the initial consonant of a case suffix, creating a potential segmentation ambiguity that the fixed ordering resolves. Provide translations for interest but state they have no bearing on the solution.

5. **Design the verb agreement paradigm.** The verb agrees with the subject for person and number. The paradigm has the following slots: 1sg, 2sg, 3sg, 1pl.incl, 1pl.excl, 2pl, 3pl. Each slot has a distinct suffix. The 3rd person is not zero (it has its own suffix). The inclusive/exclusive distinction is the key target-specific feature: there are two distinct 1st person plural forms. The semantic distinction must be recoverable from the data: in the contextual sentences or glosses, the inclusive form should appear in contexts where the addressee is included in the group (e.g., "we (you and I) went"), and the exclusive form in contexts where the addressee is not included (e.g., "we (I and others) went"). The solver must infer the semantic distinction from these contextual clues. Ensure that the inclusive and exclusive suffixes are phonologically distinct enough to be clearly identified as separate morphemes, but not so different that the solver cannot see they belong to the same paradigm slot (1pl).

6. **Design the verb data.** Present 4–6 verbs, each conjugated in a subset of the person/number slots. The critical design constraint is that no single verb is conjugated in all slots. Instead, different verbs cover different slots, so the solver must synthesize the full paradigm across multiple verbs. Ensure: (a) At least 3 verbs show the 1pl.incl form and at least 3 show the 1pl.excl form, with contextual clues distinguishing them; (b) The verb stems are different from the noun stems (to confirm the patterns are general); (c) At least one verb stem ends in a long vowel and at least one in a short vowel (to show that the vowel length contrast is present in the verbal domain as well, even if it does not condition verbal alternations); (d) The verbs are semantically transparent (e.g., "go," "eat," "see," "give") so the solver can use the semantics to help identify the inclusive/exclusive distinction. If evidentiality is included (OPTIONAL), add one additional suffix slot between tense and person/number, and show it in at least 2–3 verb forms. This adds a layer of complexity but should only be included if the puzzle needs additional difficulty beyond the inclusive/exclusive distinction.

7. **Integrate vowel length as a cross-cutting phonological feature.** Vowel length should appear in the data in at least two ways: (a) as the conditioning factor for the plural suffix alternation (Step 2), and (b) as a phonemic contrast visible in the stem inventory (at least one pair of stems that differ only in vowel length, or a stem where the length is relevant to stress). If stress is marked in the orthography or transcription (e.g., with an acute accent), ensure that long vowels attract stress in a predictable way (e.g., stress falls on the syllable containing the long vowel if it is in the stem, or on the penultimate syllable otherwise). This gives the solver an independent diagnostic for identifying long vowels, which in turn helps them identify the conditioning factor for the plural suffix alternation. If stress is not marked, the vowel length must be indicated in the transcription (e.g., with a macron or double vowel) and the solver must notice the contrast from the plural suffix alternation alone.

8. **Design the question sequence for progressive difficulty.** The questions should be ordered so that each builds on discoveries from earlier questions. Suggested structure: (Q1) Present 6–8 noun forms (a mix of singular and plural, in various cases) and ask the solver to identify which forms are plural and to segment each form into stem + suffixes. This tests whether the solver has recognized the agglutinative structure and the plural suffix. (Q2) Present 4–5 nouns with one form given (singular or plural) and the other missing, in a specific case. The solver must predict the missing form, applying both the plural suffix alternation and the case suffix. At least one item should involve a stem where the final vowel length is not immediately obvious from the given form (e.g., the given form is in a case where the stem-final vowel is obscured by the case suffix), requiring the solver to use the plural suffix alternation as a diagnostic for vowel length. (Q3) Present 4–5 verb forms with some person/number slots missing. The solver must predict the missing forms. At least one missing slot should be 1pl.incl or 1pl.excl, requiring the solver to have correctly identified the inclusive/exclusive distinction. (Q4) Present 2–3 new verb stems (not seen in the data) and ask the solver to conjugate them in all person/number slots. This tests full generalization of the agreement paradigm. (Q5) Ask the solver to explain: (a) the suffix ordering for nouns; (b) the conditioning factor for the plural suffix alternation; (c) the inclusive/exclusive distinction and how they identified it; (d) the role of vowel length in the system. This ensures the solver has a coherent theoretical account.

9. **Design controlled contrasts to distinguish competing hypotheses.** The data must be structured so that the solver can rule out alternative analyses. Key contrasts to include: (a) A contrast showing that the plural suffix is not part of the stem: two nouns with the same stem but different plural suffixes (one with a long final vowel, one with a short) should be presented side by side. (b) A contrast showing that case suffixes are not part of the plural suffix: a noun in the plural in two different cases should show the same plural suffix with different case suffixes attached. (c) A contrast showing that the inclusive/exclusive distinction is not a tense or aspect distinction: the same verb in 1pl.incl and 1pl.excl should appear in the same tense/aspect, with only the person/number suffix differing. (d) A contrast showing that vowel length is phonemic and not an allophonic variant: at least one pair of forms that are identical except for vowel length and have different meanings or different morphological behavior.

10. **Verify solvability and absence of unintended ambiguity.** Check that: (a) The plural suffix alternation is uniquely determined by the stem-final vowel length (no stem should be ambiguous between the two variants); (b) The suffix ordering (stem → plural → case) is the only consistent segmentation of all noun forms (no alternative ordering should fit the data); (c) The inclusive and exclusive 1pl forms are clearly distinguishable in the data and their semantic distinction is recoverable from context (the solver should not be able to assign inclusive/exclusive arbitrarily); (d) The verb agreement paradigm is fully reconstructable from the distributed data (every slot is attested in at least one verb); (e) No form in the data can be segmented in two ways that both fit the system (if a form is ambiguous, it should be flagged as such in the question design, not left to the solver to resolve without guidance); (f) The total number of distinct features the solver must track (vowel length, plural suffix alternation, case suffixes, suffix ordering, person/number agreement, inclusive/exclusive) is challenging but each feature is independently discoverable from the data before being required in a question.
