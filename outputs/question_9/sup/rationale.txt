

TARGET-LANGUAGE

Supyire (Senufo, Niger-Congo)

CONSTRUCTION RATIONALE

1. **Design the noun class system: phonological specifications.** Define 5–6 noun classes, each specified by a unique combination of three independent phonological parameters: (a) oral vowel quality alternation (e.g., /a/→/e/, /o/→/u/, /i/→/ɛ/), (b) nasalization alternation (e.g., oral→nasal, nasal→oral, or no change), and (c) tone alternation (e.g., H→L, L→H, or no change). Ensure that no two classes are identical across all three parameters. Deliberately create at least two pairs of classes that share one or two parameters but differ in the third (e.g., Class A and Class B share the same vowel quality change but differ in tone; Class C and Class D share the same nasalization pattern but differ in vowel quality). This partial overlap is essential: it creates the ambiguity that later questions will exploit, and it forces the solver to track all three dimensions independently rather than treating them as a single "vowel change." The solver should be able to count the distinct classes from the data but should not be told the number explicitly.

2. **Design the class-specific plural suffix paradigm.** Assign a distinct plural suffix to each class (e.g., Class 1: -ɛ, Class 2: -i, Class 3: -ɔ, Class 4: -a, Class 5: -ɛ̀, Class 6: -ɔ̀ — the setter chooses specific forms). The suffix must be phonologically simple (one or two segments) so that the solver can reliably segment it from the stem. The suffix should be tonally specified (some may carry a tone that differs from the stem's tone) to add a small additional layer: the solver must recognize that the tone on the suffix is part of the suffix, not part of the stem alternation. This step creates the decomposition challenge: the plural form is [stem with internal alternations] + [class-specific suffix], and the solver must learn to separate these two components. The suffix paradigm provides a second, independent locus of class marking that the solver can cross-check against the stem alternation pattern.

3. **Design the nominal agreement paradigm.** Create a set of agreement forms (demonstratives, adjectives, or 3rd-person pronouns — the setter chooses which nominal word classes to include) that are class-specific and number-specific. Each agreement form should be a short, transparent morpheme (1–2 syllables) that is clearly distinct across classes. The critical design principle: the same class that determines the stem alternation pattern and the plural suffix also determines the agreement form. This is the cross-paradigm correspondence. The agreement forms should be presented in a way that makes their class-specificity discoverable: for example, if using demonstratives, show "this [noun]" where the demonstrative form changes with the noun's class. If using pronouns, show a compact table of 3rd-person pronouns (singular and plural) that the solver can compare against the noun classes. The agreement paradigm should be "cleaner" than the noun paradigm (fewer phonological dimensions, shorter forms) so that it serves as a verification tool: once the solver has identified a noun's class from its stem alternations, they can check whether the agreement form matches.

4. **Select the noun vocabulary.** Provide a table of 15–22 nouns, each shown in singular and plural, with translations. Ensure: (a) Each class is represented by at least 3 examples to allow reliable induction of the alternation pattern; (b) The nouns cover a range of root vowels (all oral vowels in the inventory, plus at least one nasal vowel) so that the solver can see the vowel alternation applies across the inventory and is not idiosyncratic to one vowel; (c) Include at least 2–3 nouns whose root vowel is nasal in the singular (to establish that nasalization is a property of the root, not just an alternation output); (d) Include at least 2 nouns where the stem alternation involves a change in both oral quality AND nasalization simultaneously (e.g., /a/→/ẽ/) to make the independence of the two dimensions visible; (e) Include at least 2 nouns where the tone change is the ONLY alternation (vowel quality and nasalization are unchanged) to isolate tone as an independent parameter; (f) The translations should be semantically varied (animals, body parts, abstract nouns, etc.) and the setter should explicitly state that semantic class does not determine noun class assignment — this prevents the solver from using semantics as a shortcut and forces them to rely on phonological patterns.

5. **Select the agreement vocabulary and presentation.** Present the agreement forms in a context that makes the cross-paradigm correspondence discoverable but not immediately obvious. Two effective strategies: (Strategy A) Present a table where each noun (from a subset of the noun table) is shown alongside its agreement form in a simple phrase (e.g., "this [noun]" or "[adjective] [noun]"), so the solver can see that the agreement form changes when the noun's class changes. (Strategy B) Present a separate compact table of agreement forms (e.g., a pronoun paradigm) and ask the solver to match them to noun classes. Strategy A is more natural and progressive; Strategy B is more direct. The setter may combine both: show some agreement forms in context (Strategy A) and then present a fuller paradigm separately (Strategy B). The key is that the solver must discover that the agreement form is determined by the noun's class — this is the central "insight" of the puzzle, analogous to the verb-noun correspondence in the source rationale.

6. **Design the presentation sequence for progressive discovery.** The data should be presented in a sequence that builds understanding incrementally. Recommended structure: (Phase 1) Present the noun table (singular + plural) without any agreement forms. The solver's task at this stage is to identify the stem alternation patterns and the plural suffixes, and to group nouns into classes. This is the "hard" phase because the solver must track three phonological dimensions simultaneously. (Phase 2) Present the agreement data (demonstratives/adjectives/pronouns) alongside a subset of the nouns. The solver's task is to discover that the agreement form is class-specific and to identify the correspondence between noun class and agreement form. This is the "insight" phase. (Phase 3) Present test items (questions) that require applying both the noun patterns and the cross-paradigm correspondence. The solver should not be given explicit instructions about what to look for; the data itself should guide the inference. The setter may use section headings or formatting to signal the phases, but should not state the rules.

7. **Design Q1: Class identification and decomposition.** Present 6–8 noun forms (a mix of singular and plural, some with only one form given) and ask the solver to: (a) identify which class each noun belongs to; (b) for plural forms, identify the stem and the suffix separately. Include at least one form where the stem alternation is minimal (e.g., only a tone change) to test whether the solver is tracking tone as an independent parameter. Include at least one form where the nasalization change is the only alternation to test whether the solver recognizes nasalization as distinct from oral quality change. The distractors should include forms that violate a pattern in a subtle way (e.g., correct vowel quality but wrong tone, or correct tone but wrong nasalization) to test whether the solver has internalized all three dimensions independently.

8. **Design Q2: Cross-paradigm prediction (agreement completion).** Present 5–7 items where the solver is given a noun (singular or plural) and must predict the corresponding agreement form (or vice versa: given an agreement form, predict which noun class it belongs to and what the noun's plural form would be). This is the direct test of the cross-paradigm correspondence. Ensure: (a) At least 2 items require the solver to first identify the noun's class from its stem alternations, then look up the corresponding agreement form; (b) At least 1 item requires the reverse direction (given an agreement form, predict the noun's plural form); (c) At least 1 item involves a noun whose class is ambiguous from the stem alone (because two classes share the same vowel quality change but differ in tone or nasalization), requiring the solver to use the agreement form to resolve the ambiguity. This last item is critical: it demonstrates the practical utility of the cross-paradigm correspondence and shows that the agreement paradigm is not redundant but provides independent information.

9. **Design Q3: Noun prediction with ambiguity.** Present 8–12 nouns with one form (singular or plural) given and the other missing. Ensure the following distribution: (a) At least 3 items have a unique, unambiguous prediction (only one class fits the given form); (b) At least 2–3 items are ambiguous between two classes (because the given form is compatible with multiple class patterns — e.g., the vowel quality change is the same for two classes but the tone or nasalization is not yet determined), requiring the solver to provide multiple valid answers or to use additional context (such as an agreement form provided alongside) to resolve the ambiguity; (c) At least 1–2 items require applying the alternation rules to a root vowel not seen in the training data (testing generalization — e.g., if the training data shows /a/→/e/ but not /ɔ/→/ɛ/, the solver must generalize the pattern); (d) At least 1 item involves a nasal vowel in the root, requiring the solver to track nasalization as an independent dimension. The ambiguity items are the most important: they test whether the solver understands that the system has multiple interacting parameters and that a single surface form may be compatible with multiple underlying class assignments.

10. **Design Q4: Explanation and system description.** Ask the solver to: (a) state the number of noun classes and describe each class by its defining alternation pattern (vowel quality change, nasalization change, tone change) and its plural suffix; (b) explicitly state the cross-paradigm correspondence (i.e., that the same class determines the stem alternation, the suffix, and the agreement form); (c) explain how they resolved the ambiguous cases in Q3 (i.e., what additional information or reasoning they used). This question ensures the solver has a coherent theoretical account rather than pattern-matching by rote. It also forces the solver to articulate the independence of the three phonological dimensions, which is the deepest structural insight of the puzzle.

11. **Verify the problem for solvability and unintended issues.** Check that: (a) Every noun class is uniquely identifiable from the noun table when all three parameters are considered together (no two classes produce identical singular-plural pairs for any root); (b) The partial overlaps between classes (designed in Step 1) create genuine ambiguity in Q3 but do not make the classes impossible to distinguish from the full noun table; (c) The agreement forms are unambiguous (each class has a distinct agreement form, so the cross-paradigm correspondence is a one-to-one mapping); (d) The plural suffixes are segmentable from the stems in all cases (no phonological processes at the stem-suffix boundary that would obscure the boundary); (e) The nasal vowels are clearly marked in the notation (using tilde or another unambiguous diacritic) so that the solver can distinguish nasal from oral vowels without ambiguity; (f) The tone notation is consistent and unambiguous (using diacritics or a tone letter system that the solver can interpret from context); (g) No item in Q3 can be solved by simple analogy to a single training example without applying the abstract rule (i.e., the roots in Q3 items should differ from those in the training nouns in at least one phonological feature); (h) The total number of distinct phonological features the solver must track (oral vowel quality, nasalization, tone, suffix identity) is high enough to make the problem challenging but the data is sufficient to induce all patterns with confidence; (i) The absence of verbal agreement is maintained throughout — no verb forms should appear in the data, and the solver should not be led to expect verbal marking.
