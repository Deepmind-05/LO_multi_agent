

TARGET-LANGUAGE: Yaqui (Uto-Aztecan, Cahita)

CONSTRUCTION RATIONALE

1. **Establish the basic transitive frame and word order.** Provide a set of simple transitive sentences (at least 4–5) with different noun pairs and different verb stems, each with an English gloss. All sentences should share the same underlying structure: a subject noun, an object noun, and a verb. The verb must appear in final position in every sentence. The subject noun must appear in its bare (unmarked) form, and the object noun must carry a consistent suffix. The nouns should be semantically transparent (concrete, common nouns) so the solver can map them to the gloss without difficulty. The solver's first inference should be: the verb is the final element, and the two nouns occupy preverbal positions. At this stage, the solver should not yet be able to determine which noun is subject and which is object purely from position alone (since both precede the verb), but the consistent pattern of one form being bare and the other suffixed should be visible.

2. **Make case marking the critical key to argument identification.** Because Yaqui has no verbal person or number marking (WALS 102A-1), the verb form must be identical across all sentences regardless of who the subject or object is. This is essential: the same verb stem (with the same TAM suffix, if any) must appear in sentences with different subjects and objects. The solver cannot "read off" the subject from the verb. The only evidence for distinguishing subject from object is the case suffix. The solver should infer: the unmarked form is nominative (subject), the suffixed form is accusative (object). This inference is supported by the cross-linguistic principle that in a two-case system, the unmarked case is nominative. The setter must ensure that no other cue (e.g., a pronoun that happens to be invariant, or a semantic agent/patient distinction that is always transparent) accidentally provides an alternative path to the same answer. The case suffix should be a single, consistent morpheme across all nouns in the data.

3. **Introduce intransitive sentences to confirm the analysis.** Provide 2–3 intransitive sentences (subject + verb, no object). The subject should appear in the unmarked (nominative) form, confirming that the bare form is the subject case, not merely a "preverbal noun" form. The verb should appear in final position. This step eliminates the alternative hypothesis that the bare form is simply "the first noun" and the suffixed form is "the second noun" (i.e., a positional rather than a case-based analysis). If the solver had hypothesized a positional rule, the intransitive sentences would still be consistent with it, but the combination with transitive sentences where the same noun appears as subject in one sentence (bare) and object in another (suffixed) would force the case interpretation. The setter should include at least one noun that appears as subject in one sentence and as object in another, to make this contrast explicit.

4. **Introduce the negative particle.** Provide 2–3 negative transitive sentences and 1–2 negative intransitive sentences. In each, a small, invariant word appears between the subject and the object (in transitives) or between the subject and the verb (in intransitives). This word must be the same form in every negative sentence. Its position is preverbal and it is a separate word (not attached to the verb or to any noun). The solver should infer: this is a negative particle, it is preverbal, and it is a free word. The linear order in a negative transitive sentence is S Neg O V. The setter should ensure that the negative particle is not confused with any other element: it should be a short, distinct form that does not resemble any noun, case suffix, or verb stem in the data. The contrast between positive and negative sentences with the same subject, object, and verb (differing only in the presence of the negative particle) is the cleanest evidence.

5. **Introduce plural marking and create the suffix-ordering problem.** Provide sentences in which a noun is plural. The plural noun should carry an additional suffix beyond the stem. Critically, include at least one sentence where a plural noun is in the accusative case (i.e., it is the object of a transitive verb). This sentence will show the stem with both the plural suffix and the accusative suffix. The solver must determine the order: is it stem + plural + accusative, or stem + accusative + plural? To resolve this, the setter should provide: (a) a singular accusative noun (stem + accusative), (b) a plural nominative noun (stem + plural), and (c) a plural accusative noun (stem + plural + accusative, or stem + accusative + plural). By comparing (a) and (c), the solver can see which suffix is adjacent to the stem and which is outermost. The setter should choose a consistent order (e.g., stem + plural + case, which is typologically common) and ensure the data is unambiguous. The obligatoriness of plural marking (WALS 34A-6) means that every plural context in the data must show the plural suffix; there should be no "optional" plural marking.

6. **Introduce TAM (tense-aspect) suffixes on the verb.** Provide sentences that differ in tense or aspect (e.g., past vs. non-past, or perfective vs. imperfective). The verb should carry a suffix that correlates with the TAM value. The key constraint: the TAM suffix is the ONLY variation on the verb. There is no person or number agreement. The same verb stem + TAM suffix appears regardless of the subject or object. This serves two purposes: (a) it independently confirms that the verb is the final element (the TAM-bearing word is always last), corroborating the SOV analysis; (b) it makes the absence of person marking visible and meaningful—the solver should notice that the verb does not change when the subject changes from, say, a human to an animal, or from singular to plural. The setter should include at least two TAM values (e.g., past and non-past) and show them on the same verb stem with different arguments, making the invariance with respect to person/number explicit.

7. **Introduce a postposition.** Provide 1–2 sentences containing a spatial or relational postposition. The postposition is a separate word that follows the noun phrase it modifies. For example, a sentence meaning "The dog is under the table" would have the structure: dog (nom) table (acc) under V. The postposition "under" follows the noun "table." The solver should infer: this is a postposition (a word that follows a noun to mark a spatial/relational role). The typological correlation with OV word order (which the solver has already established) provides confirmatory support. The full linear order in a complex sentence is now: S [Neg] O [PP] V. The setter should ensure the postposition is not confused with the case suffix: it is a separate word, not a bound morpheme, and it follows the entire noun phrase (including any case suffix).

8. **(Optional) Introduce a predicative adjective to create a structural trap.** Provide 1–2 sentences where the predicate is an adjective (e.g., "The house is big," "The water is cold"). Because Yaqui encodes predicative adjectives verbally (WALS 118A-1), these sentences will have the same surface structure as intransitive sentences: a nominative noun followed by a verb-like element bearing a TAM suffix. The solver may initially parse "The house is big" as a transitive sentence with "big" as the object, but the semantics (no agent-patient relationship) and the TAM suffix on the adjective should signal that this is a stative predicate. This step tests whether the solver understands that the "verb" position can be filled by an adjective. If included, this should appear later in the data, after the solver has already established the basic transitive and intransitive patterns, so that the contrast is available.

9. **Design the question set with progressive difficulty.** The questions should be structured in layers:
   - **Layer 1 (identification):** Ask the solver to identify the word order, the case system (which form is nominative, which is accusative), and the negative particle. These are the foundational discoveries from Steps 1–4.
   - **Layer 2 (morphological analysis):** Ask the solver to identify the plural suffix, determine its order relative to the case suffix, and identify the TAM suffixes. These require the discoveries from Steps 5–6.
   - **Layer 3 (production/translation):** Provide English sentences that the solver must translate into the unknown language. These should require the simultaneous application of multiple rules. Start with simple transitive sentences (testing SOV + case), then add negation (testing SNegOV), then add plural (testing suffix ordering), then add postpositions (testing full word order S Neg O PP V), and finally add TAM (testing verb suffix selection). If the predicative adjective was included, include one translation item requiring it.
   - **Layer 4 (comprehension):** Provide 2–3 sentences in the unknown language that the solver must translate into English. These should include at least one sentence with multiple interacting features (e.g., a negative, plural, postpositional sentence) to test integrated understanding.

10. **Verify internal consistency and solvability.** Check the following:
    - Every grammatical rule the solver is expected to discover must be supported by at least two independent data points in the provided sentences (no rule should rest on a single example).
    - The suffix-ordering question (Step 5) must be resolvable: the data must contain enough forms to distinguish stem+plural+case from stem+case+plural. If the same suffix form were used for both plural and accusative (which it should not be), the puzzle would be unsolvable.
    - The negative particle must not be confused with any other element. Its form should be distinct from all nouns, suffixes, and verb stems in the data.
    - The TAM suffixes must not be confused with the case or plural suffixes. They attach to verbs, not nouns, and their forms should be distinct from the nominal suffixes.
    - The postposition must be clearly a separate word (not a suffix) and must not be confused with the negative particle. Their positions differ (postposition follows the noun it modifies; negative particle is between S and O).
    - No sentence in the data should be ambiguous under the intended analysis. If a sentence could be parsed in two ways that yield different English glosses, the setter must revise it.
    - The total number of distinct morphemes (noun stems, verb stems, suffixes, particles, postpositions) should be manageable (aim for 10–15 distinct forms total) so that the solver can track them without being overwhelmed.

11. **Control for alternative hypotheses.** The most dangerous alternative hypothesis is that the language is SVO with a post-verbal object marker (i.e., the solver might think the "accusative suffix" is actually a post-verbal object marker and the word order is SVO). To prevent this, ensure that: (a) the suffixed noun always precedes the verb (it is not post-verbal); (b) the unmarked noun always precedes the suffixed noun in transitive sentences; (c) in intransitive sentences, the unmarked noun precedes the verb with no suffixed noun present. If the solver hypothesized SVO with a post-verbal object marker, the intransitive sentences would be problematic (there would be no object marker to explain), and the consistent preverbal position of both nouns would contradict the hypothesis. A second alternative hypothesis is that the language is OSV (object before subject). To prevent this, include at least one sentence where the same noun appears as subject (unmarked) in one sentence and as object (marked) in another, making it clear that the marking, not the position, determines the grammatical role.
