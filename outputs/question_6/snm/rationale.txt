

TARGET-LANGUAGE: Sanuma (snm), Yanomam family, South America

CONSTRUCTION RATIONALE

1. **Establish the basic clause structure (SOV) through intransitive data.** Begin the puzzle data with a small set of intransitive clauses (2–3 sentences). Each should contain a single nominal argument (a noun or pronoun) followed by a verb. The semantic content should be simple and concrete (e.g., "The child sleeps," "The dog runs," "I eat"). The solver's first inference should be that the verb occupies final position and the single argument precedes it. At this stage, the solver has no reason to suspect anything unusual about the argument's role—it is simply "the thing that does the action or is in the state." Do not yet include any transitive clauses. This clean intransitive baseline is essential because it establishes the absolutive form of the argument before the ergative contrast is introduced.

2. **Introduce transitive clauses and the ergative case marker.** Add 2–3 transitive clauses to the data. The critical design constraint is this: at least one noun or pronoun that appeared as the sole argument in an intransitive clause (Step 1) must now appear as the transitive subject (A) in a transitive clause, and it must carry a visible suffixal or clitic element that was absent in the intransitive clause. Simultaneously, the transitive object (P) must appear in the same unmarked form that the intransitive subject (S) had. This creates the minimal contrast: the same lexical item is unmarked when it is S or P, but marked when it is A. The solver should be able to infer: (a) the language is SOV (confirming Step 1), (b) there is a case marker on the transitive subject, and (c) the intransitive subject and transitive object share the same (unmarked) form. This is the central ergative-absolutive inference. The case marker should be a short clitic attached to the right of the host (e.g., a one- or two-syllable element), not a full word, to support the later inference that it is a clitic rather than a postposition.

3. **Demonstrate verb invariance (no person/number agreement).** Ensure that at least one verb stem appears in multiple clauses with different subjects and objects (different persons, different numbers) without any change in form. For example, the same verb should appear with a first-person subject, a third-person subject, and a plural subject, all in identical surface form. This is critical because it eliminates the possibility that the solver uses verbal morphology to identify arguments. The solver must therefore rely entirely on the case marking on the nominals. If the verb were to change form, the solver could potentially identify the subject from the verb and the case system would become less central. The invariance should be visible but not stated explicitly—the solver discovers it by noticing that the verb does not change.

4. **Introduce the negative word in postverbal position.** Add 2–3 negative clauses to the data. Each negative clause should be a straightforward negation of a positive clause that also appears in the data (or is semantically parallel). The negative element should be a separate, invariant word that appears immediately after the verb. In an SOV clause, this yields the surface order SOVNeg. The solver must infer: (a) the negative element is a separate word (not a suffix on the verb, not a prefix), (b) it is invariant (same form regardless of the verb or subject), and (c) it occupies a fixed position immediately postverbal. To prevent the solver from mistaking the negative word for an object or a postposition, ensure that in at least one negative clause, the object is also present and clearly distinct from the negative word (i.e., the order is A-P-V-Neg, not A-V-Neg-P). The negative word should be a short, distinctive form that does not resemble any other element in the data.

5. **Introduce the three-way demonstrative system.** Add 2–3 clauses containing demonstratives. The three demonstratives should appear in contexts where a spatial or deictic distinction is inferable from the semantic content or from contextual cues in the glosses (e.g., a gloss like "this [near speaker]," "that [near listener]," "that [far from both]" or similar). All three demonstratives should precede the noun they modify. The solver should infer: (a) there are three distinct demonstrative forms, (b) they encode a three-way contrast (the exact nature of the contrast—proximal/medial/distal, or speaker/hearer/other—should be inferable from context), and (c) the demonstrative precedes the noun. The pre-nominal position is notable because it contrasts with the post-nominal position of adjectives and postpositions (see Step 8), creating a subtle internal contrast within the noun phrase that a careful solver will notice.

6. **Introduce the inclusive/exclusive distinction in first person plural.** Include at least two clauses where a first-person plural pronoun is the subject or object. In one context, the semantic content should make it clear that the addressee is included in the reference (e.g., "We [you and I] went to the river" where the addressee is the person being spoken to). In the other context, the addressee is excluded (e.g., "We [my friends and I] went to the river" where the addressee is not part of the group). The two forms should be distinct. Because the language has ergative-absolutive alignment, the solver will see these forms in both absolutive and ergative positions across the data. The solver must disentangle two dimensions simultaneously: inclusive vs. exclusive, and ergative vs. absolutive. This creates a 2×2 paradigm for first person plural. The inclusive/exclusive distinction should be inferable purely from the semantic context of the glosses, not from any morphological cue.

7. **Add supporting structural evidence for the head-final pattern.** Include 1–2 clauses that contain a postpositional phrase (a spatial or relational marker following the noun it modifies) and 1–2 clauses that contain an adjective following the noun. These elements serve two purposes: (a) they reinforce the overall head-final typology (OV, postpositions, N-Adj), giving the solver converging evidence for the SOV analysis; and (b) they create a contrast with the pre-nominal demonstrative (Step 5), which is a notable exception to the head-final pattern within the noun phrase. The postpositional phrase should appear in pre-verbal position (XOV), consistent with the overall structure. The plural marker, if included, should be a separate word following the noun (consistent with head-final tendency), and its position relative to the ergative clitic should be consistent and unambiguous (e.g., N-Plural-Ergative or N-Ergative-Plural—choose one and be consistent).

8. **Verify internal consistency and eliminate ambiguity.** Before finalizing the data, check the following: (a) The ergative clitic must never be confused with a postposition. Ensure that the clitic is always attached directly to the host with no space (or with a consistent orthographic convention indicating clitic status), while postpositions are separate words. (b) The negative word must never be confused with an object. Ensure that in every negative clause with an object, the object and the negative word are clearly distinct in form and position. (c) The three demonstratives must never be confused with each other or with other pre-nominal elements. (d) The inclusive and exclusive first-person plural forms must be clearly distinct from each other and from the second-person forms. (e) No verb form should accidentally resemble a case marker, a demonstrative, or the negative word. (f) The data should contain enough variety in lexical items that the solver cannot rely on a single noun or verb to make inferences—each structural property should be observable across multiple lexical items.

9. **Design the question set to test progressive inference.** Structure the questions in layers of increasing difficulty: (a) First, questions that test basic word-order identification (e.g., "In the following sentence, which word is the verb?" or "What is the order of the three main elements?"). These test Step 1. (b) Second, questions that test ergative alignment (e.g., "In sentence X, which argument is the agent? How do you know?" or "Why does the noun in sentence Y have a different form than the same noun in sentence Z?"). These test Steps 2–3 and are the core of the puzzle. (c) Third, questions that test the negative word (e.g., "What is the function of the word X in sentence Y?" or "Translate this English sentence into the language, including negation"). These test Step 4. (d) Fourth, questions that test the demonstrative system (e.g., "What is the difference between the three demonstratives? What does each one indicate?"). These test Step 5. (e) Fifth, questions that test the inclusive/exclusive distinction (e.g., "In which of the following sentences is the addressee included in the reference? Why?"). These test Step 6. (f) Finally, include 1–2 production questions that require the solver to combine multiple properties simultaneously (e.g., produce a negative transitive clause with an ergative-marked first-person plural inclusive subject, a demonstrative-modified object, and a postpositional phrase). These test the integration of all systems.

10. **Calibrate difficulty through controlled contrast and generalization.** The puzzle should be solvable by a solver who carefully tracks all the data, but should discriminate between solvers who notice only surface patterns and those who infer the underlying alignment system. The key difficulty driver is the interaction between ergative alignment and the absence of verbal person marking: a solver who assumes nominative-accusative alignment (the most common pattern cross-linguistically) will make errors on the ergative-related questions. A solver who notices the verb invariance will be pushed toward the case-marking system. The inclusive/exclusive distinction adds a combinatorial layer that requires the solver to track two independent dimensions in the pronoun paradigm. The pre-nominal demonstrative (contrasting with post-nominal adjectives and postpositions) adds a subtle typological detail that rewards careful observation. The overall difficulty should be such that a solver who correctly identifies the ergative-absolutive alignment and the SOV order can answer the majority of questions, but full credit requires also identifying the negation pattern, the demonstrative system, and the inclusive/exclusive distinction.
