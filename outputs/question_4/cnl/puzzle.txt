ANSWERS

**Q1**

(a) SOV (Subject–Object–Verb).

(b) The patient (P) is marked. Evidence ruling out accusative (A-marking): (1) In item 6 (*ka na-tɨp*, "He sees me"), the agent is 3P (*ka*) but the verbal prefix is *na-* (1P), matching the patient ("me"), not the agent. (2) In item 7 (*na ta-sɨk*, "I hit you"), the agent is 1P (*na*) but the prefix is *ta-* (2P), matching the patient ("you"), not the agent. In both cases, an accusative analysis would predict the prefix matches the agent, which it does not.

(c) *wa-* marks the inclusive first-person plural (speaker + addressee), while *ya-* marks the exclusive first-person plural (speaker + other, excluding addressee).

**Q2**

(a) The possessor bears the marking (as a prefix on the possessed noun).

(b) (i) The form *na* in item 19 (*na-hu*) is the same morpheme as the *na* in item 29 (*hu na*). In item 29, *na* functions as a locative postposition ("at-me"), making the sentence literally "house at-me." This reveals that the "possessive prefix" in dependent noun phrases is actually a locative marker: possession is expressed through a locational strategy ("X at-me" = "my X"), not through a dedicated possessive morpheme. (ii) *nɨ ta* (literally "dog at-you" = "You have a dog").

**Q3**

(a) WITH-language (or a language with a WITH-conjunction).

(b) (i) *wa-pɨk*. (ii) Disagree. The inability to take transitive arguments does not make adjectives a separate category; it simply means they are intransitive predicates. The critical evidence is that adjectives take the exact same P-marker prefix as verbs: item 38 (*na-tɨk*, "I am big") uses the same 1P prefix *na-* as item 1 (*na-wɨl*, "I go"), and item 42 (*ya-pɨk*, "We (excl.) are cold") uses the same exclusive 1PL prefix *ya-* as item 5 (*ya-mɨn*, "We (excl.) sleep"). This shows that the P-marking system is a general property of all predicates in Canela, not limited to a specific verb subclass. Adjectives are grammatically verbal.

SOLUTION

**Inferred rules:**

1. **SOV word order:** All main clauses follow Subject–Object–Verb order. Postpositions follow their noun phrase. This is immediately apparent from the data (e.g., item 13: *na pɨ ka-tɨp* = S O V).

2. **Ergative P-marking:** The verb carries a prefix that marks the patient (P) in transitive clauses and the sole argument (S) in intransitive clauses. The agent (A) in transitive clauses is unmarked (appears as a bare noun or pronoun). The P-marker paradigm is:
   - 1P: *na-*
   - 2P: *ta-*
   - 3P: *ka-*
   - 1PL.incl: *wa-*
   - 1PL.excl: *ya-*

   Evidence: Intransitive items 1–5 show the prefix matching the S. Transitive items 6–12 show the prefix matching the patient, not the agent (e.g., item 6: *ka* is A=3P, but prefix *na-* marks P=1P; item 12: *ya* is A=1PL.excl, but prefix *ta-* marks P=2P). Items 13–18 confirm that noun objects are 3P (prefix is *ka-*).

3. **Inclusive/exclusive distinction:** *wa-* = inclusive (speaker + addressee), *ya-* = exclusive (speaker + other). Evidenced by the glosses in items 4, 5, 11, 12.

4. **Dependent marking in possessive NPs:** The possessor is marked by a prefix on the possessed noun. The possessed noun is unmarked. The prefix forms are identical to the verbal P-marker forms.

5. **Locational possession:** Predicative possession is expressed as [possessed-noun] [possessor-locative], where the locative form is the same as the "possessive prefix." This reveals that the possessive prefix is actually a locative marker. The underlying strategy is locational: "my house" = "house at-me."

6. **WITH-language:** The postposition *ri* serves both as a comitative ("with") and a coordinative ("and"). Disambiguation is contextual: if one NP is the main agent and the other accompanies, it is comitative; if both NPs are co-agents, it is coordinative.

7. **Verbal adjectives:** Adjective predicates take the same P-marker prefix as verbs, confirming that adjectives are grammatically verbal in Canela. The P-marking system is a general property of all predicates.

**How rules produce each answer:**

- Q1(a): Directly observable from all sentences (verb is final).
- Q1(b): Requires comparing the first word (A) with the verbal prefix in transitive sentences and explicitly showing that an accusative analysis fails. Items 6 and 7 provide clear contrasts where the prefix matches P but not A.
- Q1(c): Requires mapping the two 1PL forms to their glosses (items 4, 5, 11, 12).
- Q2(a): Directly observable from items 19–28 (prefix is on the possessor).
- Q2(b)(i): Requires comparing the form *na* in its two positions (prefix in 19, postposition in 29) and recognizing the locational interpretation. Q2(b)(ii): Requires applying the locational strategy to a novel combination (dog + 2P), testing whether the solver has generalized the pattern rather than memorized examples.
- Q3(a): Requires recognizing that the same form *ri* has two functions (comitative in 34–35, coordinative in 36–37).
- Q3(b)(i): Requires combining the verbal adjective insight with the inclusive/exclusive distinction to produce a novel form. Q3(b)(ii): Requires the solver to articulate a linguistic argument using specific data points, demonstrating understanding that the P-marking system is a property of the predicate class, not a verb-specific feature.
- Q4a: Each item requires applying the relevant rule(s): 43 (ergative + inclusive), 44 (ergative + exclusive), 45 (dependent marking), 46 (locational possession), 47 (WITH-language), 48 (verbal adjective).
- Q4b: Item 49 is surprising because *na-* marks the patient (1P "me"), not the agent; a naive analysis would predict the prefix marks the agent, yielding "I throw him." The solver can confirm the meaning of *-gɨl* from item 18 (*na ka-gɨl*, "I throw him"), where *na* is the bare agent and *ka-* marks the patient. In item 49, the roles are reversed: *ka* is the bare agent (3P "he") and *na-* marks the patient (1P "me"). Item 50 is surprising because it is a predicative possession sentence (locational strategy: "fish at-me"), not a dependent possessive noun phrase; a naive analysis would interpret it as a possessive NP ("my fish"). Items 51–53 are straightforward applications of known rules.
- Q4c: Each item requires combining multiple rules: (1) ergative + noun object; (2) ergative with 1PL.incl as unmarked agent and 3P patient marked; (3) verbal adjective; (4) locational possession; (5) exclusive P-marker + WITH-language comitative; (6) ergative with 3P A and 3P P; (7) dependent marking; (8) WITH-language comitative with a noun (bɨ) attested in item 17.

DIFFICULTY

**Level 5.** The puzzle requires the solver to simultaneously decode multiple interacting systems: (1) ergative alignment (the central insight that the marked argument is the patient, not the agent), (2) the inclusive/exclusive distinction embedded within the same P-marker paradigm, (3) the two-stage inference from surface "possessive prefix" to underlying locational strategy, (4) the WITH-language property requiring contextual disambiguation, and (5) the verbal nature of adjectives. The difficulty arises from the entanglement of these concepts in the same morphological forms (the P-marker, the locative marker, and the possessive prefix are all the same set of forms), requiring the solver to maintain a unified analysis across verbal, nominal, and adjectival domains. The prediction sub-tasks in Q2(b)(ii) and Q3(b)(i) require the solver to apply multiple rules simultaneously to novel combinations, while Q3(b)(ii) demands explicit argumentation using cross-domain evidence. The production task (Q4c) demands full generalization, including items with unseen verb/noun roots and combinations of multiple rules.
