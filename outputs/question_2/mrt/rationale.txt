

TARGET-LANGUAGE: Martuthunira (mrt), Western Pama-Nyungan, Australia

CONSTRUCTION RATIONALE

1. **Choose a narrative scenario and define the participant set.** Select a short narrative or set of related sentences (8–14 sentences) about a coherent scenario involving 3–5 named participants (people, places, or objects). The scenario should naturally involve: movement between locations (to trigger allative/ablative/locative cases), giving or directing action toward someone (dative), using an instrument or means (instrumental), and at least one instance of reported or inferred information (evidentiality). The participants should include at least one first-person plural context (to trigger the inclusive/exclusive distinction) and at least one context where the addressee is clearly included or excluded from a group. This scenario provides the semantic scaffolding from which the solver will deduce case functions. The setter should ensure that each of the 5–6 target case functions (nominative, accusative, dative, locative, ablative, allative, and optionally instrumental or temporal) appears in at least two distinct sentences with different nouns, so the solver can triangulate the function of each suffix.

2. **Establish the baseline: simple SVO sentences with no case ambiguity.** Begin the data with 2–3 simple transitive sentences of the form [Noun₁] [Noun₂] [Verb] where the semantic roles (agent, patient) are unambiguous from the verb's meaning (e.g., a clear action verb like 'hit' or 'eat'). The verb form must be identical across these sentences regardless of which noun is the subject or object. This is the critical first inference: the solver should notice that the verb does not change when the subject changes, establishing the absence of verbal person/number agreement. The solver should also identify the SVO word order from these sentences. At this stage, the solver can tentatively assign nominative to the first noun and accusative to the second, but should not yet be able to confirm this without further evidence.

3. **Introduce case contrasts through controlled noun substitution.** Provide 2–3 sentences where the same noun appears in different grammatical roles across sentences, with different suffixes. For example, a noun that is the subject in one sentence (unmarked or with a specific suffix) and the object in another (with a different suffix). The key design constraint: the contrast must be between the same noun in different roles, not between different nouns in the same role. This forces the solver to attribute the suffix difference to grammatical function rather than lexical identity. The solver should now be able to confirm the nominative-accusative distinction. If the language has a zero nominative (common in Pama-Nyungan), the solver should notice that the subject noun is unmarked while the object noun carries a suffix.

4. **Introduce spatial/relational cases through the absence of adpositions.** Provide 2–3 sentences where a noun appears with a suffix that encodes a spatial or relational meaning (location, direction toward, direction from, etc.). The critical design constraint: there must be NO separate preposition or postposition word in these sentences. The spatial/relational meaning must be entirely in the suffix on the noun. The solver should notice that the language has no adpositions and that the case system is doing the work that prepositions do in English. The semantic context of the scenario (e.g., "went to the river," "came from the house," "sat at the fire") should make the spatial meaning clear, allowing the solver to map suffixes to functions. At this stage, the solver should be able to identify at least 4–5 case functions (nominative, accusative, locative, allative, ablative).

5. **Introduce the noun phrase structure: adjectives and relative clauses.** Within the narrative, include at least one noun modified by an adjective (e.g., "big dog") and at least one noun modified by a relative clause (e.g., "the dog that chased the cat"). Both must appear in the same structural position: post-nominal. The adjective should be a single word; the relative clause should contain a verb and a gap (the relativized element is absent from the clause). The critical design constraint: the formal structure of both should be identical (Noun + Modifier), so the solver cannot distinguish them by form alone. The solver should infer: (a) adjectives follow the noun (N-Adj order); (b) relative clauses are adjoined (post-nominal); (c) adjectives and relative clauses are formally the same construction. The solver can distinguish them semantically (a single word vs. a clause with a gap), but the formal identity is the key inference. The relativized noun should carry its case suffix, allowing the solver to identify the relativized role from the case on the head noun.

6. **Introduce the pronoun system with inclusive/exclusive distinction.** Include at least two sentences with first-person plural pronouns: one where the addressee is included (inclusive) and one where the addressee is excluded (exclusive). The contextual clue must be clear from the narrative (e.g., "we [inclusive] went together" vs. "we [exclusive] went without you"). The pronouns should also carry case suffixes, so the solver must simultaneously identify person/number/inclusivity AND case from the same form. The solver should infer: (a) there are two first-person plural forms; (b) one is inclusive, one is exclusive; (c) pronouns participate in the case system like other nouns. This step depends on the solver having already identified the case system (steps 3–4), because the case suffix on the pronoun must be recognized.

7. **Introduce the two possessive classes.** Include at least two nouns from different possessive classes with the same possessor (e.g., "my hand" and "my house" using different possessive suffixes). The semantic distinction between the classes should be inferable from the nouns used (e.g., body parts vs. non-body parts, or kinship terms vs. other nouns). The critical design constraint: the same possessor must appear with nouns from both classes, so the solver can observe that the difference is in the possessed noun's class, not in the possessor. The solver should infer: (a) there are two possessive suffix sets; (b) the class is a property of the possessed noun; (c) the distinguishing property is semantic (the solver should be able to hypothesize what it is, even if not fully certain).

8. **Introduce verbal morphology: tense and evidentiality.** The narrative should include verbs in at least two different tenses (e.g., past and non-past) and at least one verb with an evidential suffix (marking reported or inferred information). The tense suffixes and the evidential suffix should be segmentable (concatenative). The evidential context should be clear from the narrative framing (e.g., a character reporting what someone else said, or inferring something from evidence). The solver should infer: (a) there are at least two (ideally three) tense values marked by verbal suffixes; (b) there is a separate evidential suffix; (c) these suffixes are ordered in a fixed sequence. The segmentation of tense and evidentiality depends on the solver having already identified the verb stem (from steps 2–4), so this step builds on earlier discoveries.

9. **Design the question set: comprehension and analysis.** Create questions that test the solver's understanding at multiple levels:
   - **Level 1 (case identification):** Given a new sentence (or a sentence from the data), identify the grammatical or spatial function of a specific case suffix. This tests whether the solver has correctly mapped suffixes to functions.
   - **Level 2 (sentence parsing):** Given a complex sentence (containing a relative clause, a possessive, and multiple case-marked nouns), provide a full structural analysis or translation. This tests the interaction of multiple concepts simultaneously.
   - **Level 3 (production):** Given a meaning in English (or a description), produce the target-language sentence. This tests whether the solver can apply all the rules in combination: correct case selection, correct word order, correct noun phrase structure, correct verbal morphology.
   - **Level 4 (metalinguistic):** Ask the solver to explain a pattern (e.g., "Why does this noun have a different suffix here than in sentence 3?" or "What is the difference between the two first-person plural forms?"). This tests whether the solver has formed explicit rules rather than just pattern-matching.

   The questions should progress from Level 1 to Level 4 in difficulty. Level 1 questions should be answerable from a single sentence; Level 2 questions should require integrating information across the noun phrase; Level 3 questions should require applying multiple rules simultaneously; Level 4 questions should require explicit metalinguistic reasoning.

10. **Design controlled contrasts to prevent alternative hypotheses.** At each stage, verify that the data rules out the most likely competing hypotheses:
    - **Against verbal agreement:** Ensure that at least two different subjects (different persons or numbers) appear with the identical verb form. If the verb form were to change, the solver might hypothesize agreement.
    - **Against adpositions:** Ensure that no word in the data could be interpreted as a preposition or postposition. All spatial/relational meaning must be in the case suffix. If a word could be reanalyzed as an adposition, the solver might not infer the full case system.
    - **Against adjective/relative clause distinction:** Ensure that the adjective and the relative clause are formally identical (same position, same structure). If they were formally different, the solver would not need to infer the collapse.
    - **Against a single possessive class:** Ensure that the same possessor appears with nouns from both classes. If different possessors were used, the solver might attribute the difference to the possessor rather than the possessed noun.
    - **Against evidentiality = aspect or tense:** Ensure that the evidential suffix appears on verbs of different tenses, so the solver cannot confuse it with a tense value. Also ensure that the contextual framing clearly indicates reported/inferred information rather than, e.g., habitual aspect.

11. **Ensure the inference chain is progressive and each step depends on the previous.** The solver's reasoning should follow this dependency chain:
    - Step 2 (SVO, no agreement) → enables Step 3 (case identification, because the solver knows which position is subject and which is object)
    - Step 3 (nominative/accusative) → enables Step 4 (spatial cases, because the solver now knows how to identify the "base" form of a noun)
    - Step 4 (case system) → enables Step 6 (pronoun case, because the solver can recognize case suffixes on pronouns)
    - Step 4 (case system) → enables Step 5 (relative clause gap identification, because the case on the head noun tells the solver which role is relativized)
    - Steps 2–4 (verb identification, case system) → enable Step 8 (verbal morphology segmentation, because the solver knows where the verb is and what its stem is)
    - Step 7 (possessive classes) is relatively independent but benefits from Step 4 (the solver recognizes that the possessive suffix is a type of case-like marking)

    If any step in this chain is missing or unclear, the dependent steps become unsolvable. The setter should verify that each step provides sufficient evidence for the next.

12. **Manage difficulty through concept interaction, not through obscurity.** The puzzle should be challenging because multiple concepts interact, not because individual concepts are hard to identify. For example:
    - A sentence with a relative clause modifying a case-marked noun, where the relativized element is the object, and the head noun is in the dative case: the solver must simultaneously identify the relative clause (post-nominal modifier with a gap), the case on the head noun (dative), and the gap role (object, from the case that would have been on the missing element).
    - A sentence with an inclusive first-person plural pronoun in the ablative case, with an evidential suffix on the verb: the solver must identify the pronoun (first person plural inclusive), its case (ablative), and the verbal suffix (evidential, not tense).
    - A possessive construction where the possessed noun is in a spatial case: the solver must identify the possessive class, the possessor, and the case on the possessed noun simultaneously.

    These interactions create genuine difficulty without requiring the solver to guess or rely on prior knowledge.

13. **Maintain internal consistency and avoid irregularities.** Every case suffix must have a consistent function throughout the data. Every possessive suffix must be consistent with its class. The word order must be consistently SVO (any deviation must be clearly motivated, e.g., topicalization, and should not appear in the baseline data). The verbal morphology must be consistently segmentable. The only "irregularity" in the system should be the two possessive classes, which is itself a rule-governed pattern. No arbitrary exceptions should be introduced. If the solver encounters a form that does not fit the pattern, it should be because they have not yet identified the correct rule, not because the language is irregular.

14. **Final verification checklist.** Before finalizing the puzzle, verify:
    - (a) Every case function that appears in the data is identifiable from context (the semantic scenario makes the function clear).
    - (b) The absence of verbal agreement is unambiguous (at least two different subjects with the same verb form).
    - (c) The absence of adpositions is unambiguous (no word could be reanalyzed as a preposition/postposition).
    - (d) The adjective/relative clause collapse is observable (both appear in the same position with the same form).
    - (e) The inclusive/exclusive distinction is resolvable from context (the narrative makes clear who is included/excluded).
    - (f) The two possessive classes are observable (same possessor, different suffixes, different noun types).
    - (g) The tense/evidentiality segmentation is possible (the suffixes are in a fixed order and appear in combinations that allow segmentation).
    - (h) No question requires knowledge not derivable from the data.
    - (i) The total number of distinct rules the solver must apply is manageable (aim for 6–8 core rules: SVO, no agreement, case system, N-Adj order, adjective/relative clause collapse, inclusive/exclusive, two possessive classes, tense+evidentiality).
    - (j) The puzzle is solvable by a person with no prior knowledge of Martuthunira or Australian languages, using only general linguistic reasoning and the provided data.
