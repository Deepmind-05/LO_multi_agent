

TARGET-LANGUAGE

Mono (mno), Uto-Aztecan, Northern Uto-Aztecan, California, USA

CORE

The source concept "Base 6" (Numeral Bases, 144A-5) is DROPPED. Mono, as a Uto-Aztecan language, uses a base-10 (decimal) numeral system. There is no natural or well-attested base-6 system in Mono, and forcing one would violate the solver standard. No source concepts are directly applicable to Mono.

TARGET-SPECIFIC

1. Head-final word order (SOV, postpositions, GenN, NRel)
   - Phenomenon: Consistent head-finality across clause structure (SOV), adpositions (postpositions), genitive (GenN), and relative clauses (NRel).
   - Intended solver inference: From multiple sentences, solver identifies that the verb is final, modifiers precede heads, and the language is systematically head-final.
   - Interaction with CORE: None (replaces dropped source concept as the central structural insight).
   - Difficulty contribution: Provides the foundational word-order framework against which other elements (negation, question particle) are interpreted. Low-moderate difficulty on its own; becomes more interesting in interaction with negation.
   - Priority: REQUIRED

2. Suffixing morphology with possessive prefix asymmetry
   - Phenomenon: Mono is strongly suffixing (case, plural, demonstrative, definite, tense-aspect all suffixal), but possessive marking is prefixal on the noun.
   - Intended solver inference: Solver discovers that most morphology is suffixal, then notices the exception that possession is prefixal. This asymmetry is a clear, discoverable pattern.
   - Interaction with CORE: None directly, but the suffixing pattern interacts with noun suffix stacking (concept 5).
   - Difficulty contribution: Moderate. Requires the solver to catalog morphological positions and notice the single exception. The asymmetry is a memorable, testable insight.
   - Priority: REQUIRED

3. NegSOV: negative particle precedes subject
   - Phenomenon: The negative particle appears before the subject (clause-initial), not adjacent to the verb. WALS confirms: NegSOV, preverbal negative word, no postverbal negative, no SNegOV, no SONegV, no SOVNeg.
   - Intended solver inference: Solver initially expects negation to be preverbal (adjacent to verb, as in many SOV languages) but discovers from data that it is clause-initial, before the subject. This is a counter-expectation that creates genuine reasoning.
   - Interaction with CORE: Directly interacts with head-final word order. In a head-final language, the "default" expectation is preverbal negation; Mono violates this by placing negation before the subject. This contrast is the puzzle's central tension.
   - Difficulty contribution: High. The solver must resist the typological expectation and let the data override it. This is the key "aha" moment of the puzzle.
   - Priority: REQUIRED

4. Question particle in second position
   - Phenomenon: The polar question particle occupies second position in the clause (WALS 92A-3).
   - Intended solver inference: Solver discovers that the question particle has a fixed positional slot (second constituent), not simply attached to the verb or clause-final.
   - Interaction with CORE: Interacts with SOV word order. In a sentence "S O V?", the question particle goes after the first constituent (the subject), yielding "S Q O V". This creates a word-order pattern that is neither simply preverbal nor clause-final.
   - Difficulty contribution: Moderate-high. Requires the solver to identify a positional constraint that is independent of the basic SOV order.
   - Priority: HIGH

5. Agglutinative noun suffix stacking (case, plural, demonstrative, definite)
   - Phenomenon: Nouns can bear multiple suffixes: case, plural, demonstrative, definite article. These stack in a fixed order.
   - Intended solver inference: From examples with different combinations of these features, solver determines the relative order of suffixes.
   - Interaction with CORE: Extends the suffixing pattern (concept 2) into a more complex morphological system. The fixed suffix order is a discoverable sub-pattern.
   - Difficulty contribution: Moderate. Requires multiple examples with different feature combinations to triangulate the order.
   - Priority: HIGH

6. Tense-aspect suffixes on verb
   - Phenomenon: Verbs take suffixes encoding tense and/or aspect.
   - Intended solver inference: Solver identifies which verb-final suffixes encode temporal/aspectual information.
   - Interaction with CORE: Part of the general suffixing pattern. Provides additional data points for the verb's morphological structure.
   - Difficulty contribution: Low-moderate. Straightforward once the suffixing pattern is established.
   - Priority: OPTIONAL

INTERACTIONS

1. Head-finality × NegSOV:
   - Concepts: Head-final word order (1) + NegSOV (3)
   - Interaction: In a head-final language, the typological expectation is preverbal negation (adjacent to the verb). Mono instead places negation clause-initially, before the subject. This creates a clear contrast between expectation and reality.
   - Additional inference enabled: The solver must use the head-final pattern as a baseline expectation, then notice that negation violates it. This makes the NegSOV discovery more salient and memorable.

2. Head-finality × Question particle 2nd position:
   - Concepts: Head-final word order (1) + Question particle (4)
   - Interaction: The question particle's second-position slot interacts with SOV order. In "S O V", the particle goes after S, yielding "S Q O V". This is neither clause-initial (like negation) nor clause-final (like the verb).
   - Additional inference enabled: The solver must distinguish three different positional strategies: clause-initial (negation), second-position (question particle), and clause-final (verb). This creates a rich positional typology within a single language.

3. Suffixing × Possessive prefix:
   - Concepts: Suffixing morphology (2) + Possessive prefix (2)
   - Interaction: The general suffixing pattern makes the possessive prefix stand out as a clear exception. The solver first establishes "everything is suffixal" and then discovers the exception.
   - Additional inference enabled: The solver can test whether the prefix is truly an exception or whether there are other prefixal elements. This creates a verification step.

4. Noun suffix stacking × Head-finality:
   - Concepts: Suffix stacking (5) + Head-finality (1)
   - Interaction: The suffix order on nouns is consistent with the head-final pattern (modifiers follow the head noun). The fixed order of suffixes provides a micro-level reflection of the macro-level head-finality.
   - Additional inference enabled: The solver can use the head-final principle as a heuristic for predicting suffix order, then verify against data.

CONSTRAINTS

1. The puzzle must include at least 3-4 negative sentences to clearly establish NegSOV and rule out SNegOV, SONegV, and SOVNeg alternatives. The negative particle must be a separate word (not a clitic or affix) to make its position unambiguous.

2. The question particle must appear in at least 2-3 sentences with different first constituents (e.g., subject-initial, and possibly a fronted object or adverb) to demonstrate that it is truly "second position" and not simply "after the subject."

3. The possessive prefix must be clearly distinguishable from other prefixal elements. If possible, include a sentence where a possessive-marked noun also bears a suffix (e.g., case or plural) to show the prefix-suffix co-occurrence.

4. Noun suffix order examples must include at least 3 different combinations of {case, plural, demonstrative, definite} to allow the solver to triangulate the order. At least one example should have 3+ suffixes simultaneously.

5. The puzzle must NOT include any numeral system as a central element. If numbers appear incidentally, they should be in base-10 and not draw attention to the numeral system.

6. All glosses must be consistent and use standard Leipzig Glossing Rules. The solver should be able to work from glosses alone without any prior knowledge of Mono.

7. The head-final pattern must be demonstrated across at least two different domains (e.g., clause structure AND either postpositions or relative clauses) to establish it as a language-wide tendency rather than a single construction.
