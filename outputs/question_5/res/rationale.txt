

TARGET-LANGUAGE: Resígaro (Arawakan, Japura-Colombia)

CONSTRUCTION RATIONALE

1. **Select the numeral base and construction type.** Choose a base *b* (recommended: 5 or 6) and a mixed construction scheme: values 1 through *b* are atomic (irreducible lexical items); values *b*+1 through 2*b*−1 are formed additively as [b] + [n] (e.g., "six" = "five" + "one"); values 2*b*, 3*b*, … are formed multiplicatively as [n] × [b] (e.g., "ten" = "two" × "five"); values 2*b*+1 through 3*b*−1 are mixed as [n] × [b] + [m]. The mixed scheme is essential: it gives the solver multiple structural patterns to discover and makes the base uniquely identifiable (a purely additive system would be harder to pin down, and a purely multiplicative one would be too simple). The base must be large enough that at least three distinct construction types appear in the data (atomic, additive, multiplicative) but small enough that the full range 1 through 2*b*+2 can be presented without an unwieldy table.

2. **Create the atomic numeral lexicon.** Generate *b* phonologically opaque, monomorphemic words for values 1 through *b*. Each should be 1–2 syllables, mutually dissimilar (no shared segments, no obvious phonological relationships), and distinct from all noun forms that will appear in the puzzle. These words are the "atoms" from which all compound numerals are built. The solver must discover their values purely from context and structural analysis.

3. **Create the noun lexicon and define the plural suffix.** Select 3–5 short, phonologically opaque nouns. Define a single, consistent plural suffix (1–2 consonants or a short vowel sequence) that attaches to the noun stem. The suffix appears if and only if the preceding numeral has value greater than 1. This is the critical morphological signal: it gives the solver an independent, non-semantic way to confirm which pre-noun words are numerals (the word that triggers the suffix is a numeral > 1; the one pre-noun word that does not trigger it is "one"). The suffix must be phonologically distinct from any numeral word and from any possessive prefix (if included).

4. **(Optional, for added difficulty) Define possessive prefixes.** If including this layer, create 2–3 short prefixes (e.g., for 1st, 2nd, 3rd person) that attach to the noun stem, preceding the noun root. The structure of a possessed plural phrase is: [poss-prefix + noun-stem + plural-suffix], with the numeral appearing before this entire complex noun. The possessive prefix must be phonologically distinct from the plural suffix and from all numeral words. This layer tests the solver's ability to segment a multi-morpheme noun and correctly identify the numeral as a separate, preceding element. Include this only if the puzzle needs additional morphological parsing depth; omit it if the numeral-base inference should remain the sole central challenge.

5. **Define the surface syntax of noun phrases and sentences.** Noun phrase structure: [numeral] [noun] (where noun = stem + optional plural suffix, or poss-prefix + stem + optional plural suffix). If including full sentences, use SOV order: [subject-phrase] [object-phrase] [verb]. The verb should be a simple, content-neutral word (e.g., a generic "have" or "see") that does not carry morphological information relevant to the puzzle. The SOV frame serves to embed noun phrases in a naturalistic context and to provide clause-boundary cues, but it should not add a new inference challenge beyond what the noun phrases already provide.

6. **Generate the data set with controlled coverage.** The data must include noun phrases (or sentences containing noun phrases) for values spanning at least 1 through 2*b*+2. This range is critical: it includes all atomic numerals (1–*b*), at least two additive compounds (*b*+1, *b*+2), at least one multiplicative compound (2*b*), and at least one mixed compound (2*b*+1 or 2*b*+2). Distribute these values across the different nouns so that the solver can see the same numeral with different nouns (confirming the numeral is a free word, not a bound morpheme) and the same noun with different numerals (confirming the plural suffix is triggered by the numeral, not the noun). Randomize the order of items in the presentation so the solver cannot exploit sequential numbering.

7. **Design the first question tier: lexical identification.** Ask the solver to identify, from the data, which words function as numerals and which as nouns, and to state the rule governing the suffix on nouns. The evidence for this is: (a) a small set of words consistently appears in pre-noun position; (b) all but one of these pre-noun words co-occur with a suffix on the noun; (c) the one that does not trigger the suffix is consistent across all its occurrences. The solver should be able to state: "The pre-noun words are numerals; the suffix marks plural (numeral > 1); the pre-noun word that does not trigger the suffix is 'one.'" This tier is solvable by pattern observation alone, without any knowledge of the numeral base. It establishes the vocabulary classes needed for the next tier.

8. **Design the second question tier: base identification.** Ask the solver to determine the base of the numeral system and to explain how compound numerals are constructed. The solver now has the full set of numeral words (from tier 1) and must analyze their internal structure. The key evidence: some numeral words are transparently composed of other numeral words (e.g., the word for *b*+1 contains the word for *b* followed by the word for 1). The solver must: (a) identify which numeral words are atomic vs. compound; (b) find the "pivot" value *b* at which the construction pattern shifts from atomic to compound; (c) determine the construction rule for each range (additive above *b*, multiplicative at multiples of *b*, mixed in between). The base is uniquely determined because the pivot value is the smallest value whose numeral is compound, and the additive decomposition of *b*+1 directly reveals *b*.

9. **Design the third question tier: application and encoding.** Provide items that require the solver to apply the discovered system to values not present in the original data. Include: (a) a compound numeral from the target language to be converted to an Arabic numeral (testing whether the solver correctly applies the construction rules to a novel combination); (b) an Arabic numeral to be converted into the target language (testing whether the solver can produce the correct compound form, including choosing the right construction type for the value's range); (c) if possessive prefixes were included, a possessed phrase to be parsed or produced (testing segmentation of the multi-morpheme noun alongside numeral identification). At least one item should involve a value in the mixed range (2*b*+1 to 3*b*−1) to test the most complex construction type.

10. **Verify uniqueness and prevent alternative interpretations.** Check the following: (a) No atomic numeral word is a substring of or phonologically confusable with any other numeral word or any noun word. (b) The base is uniquely determined: verify that no alternative base (e.g., *b*−1 or *b*+1) can simultaneously account for all compound numeral decompositions in the data. (c) The plural suffix is unambiguous: it never appears on a noun in a context without a numeral > 1, and it is never confused with a possessive prefix (if included). (d) The "one" numeral is uniquely identifiable: it is the only pre-noun word that never triggers the plural suffix, and it appears in at least two different noun phrases to confirm consistency. (e) If possessive prefixes are included, verify that no possessive prefix is phonologically identical to or a substring of any numeral word, so the solver cannot missegment a possessed phrase.

11. **Calibrate the difficulty progression.** The puzzle should develop in three clear stages of increasing cognitive demand. Stage 1 (tier 1) requires only pattern observation: the solver identifies word classes and a morphological rule without any arithmetic. Stage 2 (tier 2) requires structural analysis: the solver must decompose compound words, recognize the pivot, and infer the construction rules—this is the central intellectual challenge. Stage 3 (tier 3) requires productive application: the solver must use the inferred rules to handle novel inputs, which tests whether the rules were truly understood or merely memorized from the data. The transition from stage 1 to stage 2 should feel natural (the solver has already isolated the numerals and now "looks inside" them). The transition from stage 2 to stage 3 should feel like a test of mastery (the solver must generate, not just recognize). If the puzzle feels too easy at stage 2, increase the range of values presented (e.g., go up to 3*b* instead of 2*b*+2) to add more compound forms for the solver to analyze. If it feels too hard, reduce the range or remove the possessive prefix layer.
