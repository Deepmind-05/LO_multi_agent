

TARGET-LANGUAGE: Innamincka (inn), Pama-Nyungan, Central Pama-Nyungan, Australia

CONSTRUCTION RATIONALE

1. **Establish the numeral system: base 5, zero-omitting positional notation.** Choose base 5 (typologically attested in Australian languages). The system uses a digit set of size 4 (values 1–4; no explicit zero token) and positional markers for ×5, ×25, and ×125. The units place (×1) is unmarked: a bare digit at the end of a numeral expression encodes its face value. A positional marker appearing without a preceding digit encodes an implicit value of 1 at that position (e.g., the ×25 marker alone means 1×25 = 25 in decimal). Zero-valued positions are omitted entirely from the surface form.

2. **Assign distinct lexical forms to numeral tokens.** Create four digit tokens (for values 1, 2, 3, 4) and three positional-marker tokens (for ×5, ×25, ×125). All seven forms must be phonologically opaque (no internal morphological structure), short (1–2 syllables), and mutually dissimilar. Critically, the digit token for value 3 is *yala*. No other lexical item in the entire puzzle (noun, adjective, verb, clitic) may use the form *yala* or any form that is a substring of or substring-in of *yala*. The positional markers must be phonologically distinct from all digit tokens so the solver cannot confuse the two classes.

3. **Resolve the critical lexical conflict: adjective "new" ≠ numeral N3.** The adjective "new" must be assigned a form that is clearly distinct from *yala*. Use *nala* (initial /n/ vs. /y/, same vowel pattern, plausible Pama-Nyungan shape). This form is phonologically similar enough to be typologically natural but distinct enough that no solver will confuse it with the numeral. All other adjectives, nouns, and verbs in the lexicon must also be distinct from *yala* and from each other. The setter must verify the full lexicon for accidental substring relationships or near-homophony before finalizing.

4. **Define the surface syntax of numeral expressions within noun phrases.** Numerals follow the noun (NNum order). A numeral expression is a linear sequence of [digit][marker] pairs followed by a final bare digit (the units value), all appearing after the noun. Example structure: [noun-stem][plural-suffix][case-clitic][digit₁][marker₁][digit₂][marker₂]…[digitₙ]. The entire numeral expression is a post-nominal modifier. The solver must first identify the noun (using case marking and position) before parsing the numeral sequence that follows it.

5. **Design the morphological system: layered right-branching suffixes and clitics.** All inflectional morphology is suffixal. The right edge of a noun phrase has the following fixed order: [stem] + [plural suffix] + [case clitic]. The plural suffix is a bound morpheme (e.g., a short suffix like *-ri* or *-lu*); the case clitic is a separate but attached element (e.g., *-nga* for dative, *-wa* for genitive, zero for nominative/absolutive). The solver must determine this ordering from distributional evidence: forms showing stem+plural+case, stem+case (singular), and stem alone (singular, unmarked case) allow the solver to isolate each element. The case clitic is the outermost element (rightmost), the plural suffix is inner.

6. **Establish clause structure: SOV with clause-initial negation.** Clauses follow Subject–Object–Verb order. The verb is the final element in the clause and carries tense-aspect suffixes (e.g., *-na* for past, zero for present). The negative word is a separate, uninflected clitic or word that appears at the absolute beginning of the clause, before the subject. It is NOT adjacent to the verb. This creates a typological contrast: the language is consistently right-branching (modifiers follow heads, suffixes attach right, postpositions follow), but negation is a clause-initial exception. The solver must discover that the negative word's position is fixed (clause-initial) and not determined by proximity to the verb.

7. **Generate the data set: a mixed table of numeral expressions and full sentences.** Create two types of data items:
   (a) **Numeral-only items:** A set of noun phrases containing numeral expressions, presented without full sentence context (or with minimal context). These allow the solver to focus on the numeral system in isolation. Include: at least one expression that is a single positional marker (revealing the implicit-1 rule); at least one expression with a gap between markers (revealing zero omission); at least one expression using all four digit values; and at least one expression spanning three positional orders.
   (b) **Full sentences:** A set of complete SOV clauses that incorporate noun phrases with numerals, adjectives, case marking, and (in some cases) negation. These allow the solver to discover word order, morphology, and the negation pattern. Include: at least two affirmative sentences (to establish SOV and right-branching NP structure); at least two negative sentences (to establish clause-initial negation); sentences with both singular and plural nouns (to reveal the plural suffix); sentences with different case markings (to reveal the clitic system); and at least one sentence containing the adjective "new" (*nala*) in a context where the solver might initially confuse it with the numeral *yala* (to test whether the solver can distinguish them based on distributional context).

8. **Design the first question tier: structural discovery.** Ask the solver to identify: (a) which element in each sentence is the verb (testing SOV recognition); (b) which elements are nouns vs. modifiers (testing NNum/NAdj recognition); (c) what the negative word is and where it appears (testing clause-initial negation). These questions should be answerable from the full-sentence data alone, without requiring numeral decoding. This tier establishes the macro-structure before the solver tackles the numeral system.

9. **Design the second question tier: numeral decoding.** Ask the solver to convert each numeral expression in the data table to its Arabic numeral equivalent. This forces the solver to: (a) identify which tokens are digits vs. positional markers (using the fact that digits appear in multiple positional contexts while markers appear in fixed positions); (b) determine the value of each digit (using arithmetic consistency across expressions); (c) determine the base (the base is uniquely determined by the set of expressions—verify that no other base satisfies all expressions simultaneously); (d) recognize the zero-omission rule (from expressions with gaps) and the implicit-1 rule (from single-marker expressions).

10. **Design the third question tier: encoding and generalization.** Provide items that require the solver to: (a) convert Arabic numerals into Innamincka numeral expressions (testing whether the solver can produce correct forms, including cases requiring zero-omission and the implicit-1 convention); (b) produce full sentences with specified meanings (testing integration of all discovered rules: SOV, NNum, NAdj, suffixing, case clitics, negation); (c) translate a sentence containing the adjective "new" (*nala*) into the target language, ensuring the solver does not confuse it with the numeral *yala*. Include at least one value that requires the highest positional order (×125) and at least one value with an internal zero.

11. **Verify solvability and eliminate alternative interpretations.** Check the following: (a) No digit token is a substring of or phonologically similar to any positional marker token. (b) The data set includes at least two expressions where the same digit token appears in different positional contexts, confirming it is a digit (not a marker). (c) At least one expression contains a positional marker alone (no preceding digit), uniquely identifying the implicit-1 rule. (d) The base is uniquely determined: verify that no other base (4, 6, 7, 8, 10) can simultaneously satisfy all numeral expressions in the table. (e) The adjective *nala* and the numeral *yala* appear in sufficiently different syntactic contexts (adjective modifies a noun directly; numeral appears in a multi-token sequence with positional markers) that the solver can distinguish them without ambiguity. (f) The negative word does not appear in any position other than clause-initial in the data. (g) All case clitics and the plural suffix appear in consistent positions relative to the stem (no exceptions).

12. **Calibrate difficulty progression.** The first tier (structural discovery) should be solvable by simple observation: the verb is always last, the negative word is always first, modifiers always follow nouns. The second tier (numeral decoding) should require systematic elimination: the single-marker expression gives one positional value immediately; expressions with two tokens let the solver test candidate bases; the full set confirms the base and the zero-omission rule. The third tier (encoding and generalization) should require the solver to handle novel combinations: values not seen in the table, zero-omission in new positions, the implicit-1 rule in new contexts, and the integration of all morphological and syntactic rules into a single output. The final item should be the most complex (most positional orders, multiple zeros, full sentence with negation, plural, and case marking) to serve as a capstone test.
