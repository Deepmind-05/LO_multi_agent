

TARGET-LANGUAGE: Abkhaz (abk)

CONSTRUCTION RATIONALE

1. **Establish the morphological inventory and vocabulary set.**
   Select 5–7 countable nouns spanning at least two semantic categories (e.g., kinship terms like "child, mother, brother" and natural/cultural objects like "house, path, boat"). Select 4–6 adjectives that can naturally modify these nouns (e.g., "big, old, new, good, long"). Establish the possessive prefix paradigm for at least four person/number values (1SG, 2SG, 3SG, 1PL or 2PL — choose whichever set gives the clearest contrast). Establish the plural suffix form. If postpositions are included, select 1–2 (e.g., a locative "in/on" and a directional "to/toward"). Optionally include 2–3 verbs for SOV data, but keep verbal data minimal and clearly separable from the nominal-domain focus.

2. **Construct the first data block: possessive prefix discovery (the primary "aha").**
   Present 3–4 sentences (or noun-phrase fragments) in which the SAME noun stem appears with different possessive prefixes, and the possessor is NOT a separate word. The critical design principle: the solver must be able to identify the invariant portion (the noun stem) and the variable portion (the prefix) purely from distributional evidence. For example, if the noun "house" appears as [prefix-A + stem] meaning "my house" and [prefix-B + stem] meaning "your house," the solver can isolate the stem and recognize the prefix as the possessive marker. Ensure that no sentence in this block contains a separate possessor word — the possessor is encoded ONLY by the prefix. This is the moment the solver discovers head marking: the possessor is not a free element; it is fused to the head.

3. **Construct the second data block: noun-adjective order (the stable anchor).**
   Present 2–3 examples showing a noun modified by an adjective, with the noun clearly preceding the adjective. The noun should be identifiable as the head (e.g., it can take a possessive prefix in another example, or its semantic category is clearly nominal). The adjective should be a separate word following the noun. This establishes the head-first pattern in the nominal domain and gives the solver a stable reference point: "the noun comes first, the modifier comes after." This pattern will later contrast with the possessive strategy.

4. **Construct contrastive evidence: forcing unique identification of the prefix vs. the stem.**
   Present a near-minimal pair or small set where the same noun stem appears with two different possessive prefixes, and also appears WITHOUT a possessive prefix (e.g., in a 3SG context if 3SG is unmarked, or in a context where the possessor is not specified). This forces the solver to confirm: (a) the prefix is the variable element encoding the possessor, (b) the stem is invariant, (c) the prefix is not a separate word (it is orthographically fused to the stem). If 3SG is unmarked in Abkhaz, this is a natural and elegant way to show the "zero" form. If all persons have overt prefixes, use a context where the noun appears without any prefix (e.g., in a non-possessive context) to establish the bare stem.

5. **Introduce the plural suffix: the prefix-suffix sandwich.**
   Present 2–3 examples where a noun bears BOTH a possessive prefix AND a plural suffix, creating the structure [poss-prefix + stem + plural-suffix]. The key design principle: the plural suffix must be distinguishable from the possessive prefix by its position (suffix vs. prefix) and by its invariance across different possessors (the suffix stays the same whether the possessor is 1SG or 2SG, while the prefix changes). The solver should infer: "The language uses both prefixing and suffixing on the same noun; the prefix encodes the possessor, the suffix encodes plurality of the possessed." This is the second major "aha" — the noun is a morphological sandwich.

6. **Introduce postpositions: the systematic contrast with possessive marking.**
   Present 2–3 examples showing a noun followed by a postposition (e.g., "house + in" = "in the house"). The postposition is a SEPARATE word, just like the adjective in step 3. The solver should now be able to recognize a systematic pattern: adjectives and postpositions are free, post-nominal elements, while the possessor is a bound prefix on the noun. This is the third "aha": the language treats possession morphologically (fused to the head) while treating other modification syntactically (separate post-nominal words). The solver should be able to articulate this contrast.

7. **Construct complex combination sentences.**
   Present 3–4 sentences that combine multiple systems simultaneously:
   - A possessive NP with a plural suffix AND an adjective: [poss-prefix + stem + plural-suffix] + adjective. This tests whether the solver can correctly segment all three morphological/syntactic elements.
   - A possessive NP with a postposition: [poss-prefix + stem] + postposition. This tests whether the solver can distinguish the fused possessive prefix from the free postposition.
   - A sentence with two different possessors and the same noun type, showing that the prefix changes while the stem and any suffix remain constant.
   - Optionally, a simple SOV sentence with a possessive NP as an argument, to show that the possessive NP functions as a unit within the clause.

8. **Design the translation questions with progressive difficulty.**
   - Q1 (easy): Target-to-English. A simple possessive NP with a 1SG or 2SG prefix. Tests: prefix identification, stem identification. All elements appear individually in the data.
   - Q2 (easy): Target-to-English. A noun + adjective sequence. Tests: noun-adjective order. The solver must identify which word is the noun and which is the adjective.
   - Q3 (medium): Target-to-English. A possessive NP with a plural suffix. Tests: prefix-suffix segmentation. The solver must identify both the possessive prefix and the plural suffix and determine which is which.
   - Q4 (medium): Target-to-English. A possessive NP with a postposition. Tests: distinguishing the fused possessive prefix from the free postposition. The solver must recognize that the prefix is part of the noun while the postposition is a separate word.
   - Q5 (medium-hard): English-to-Target. A sentence requiring the solver to apply the correct possessive prefix + plural suffix + adjective in the correct order. The solver must select the right prefix for the given possessor, add the plural suffix, and place the adjective after the noun.
   - Q6 (hard): English-to-Target. A sentence requiring a possessive NP with a postposition AND an adjective, testing the solver's ability to correctly order all elements: [poss-prefix + stem (+ plural-suffix)] + adjective + postposition (or the correct linear order as determined by the data).
   - Q7 (hard): English-to-Target. A sentence with two possessive NPs (different possessors, same or different nouns) in a single clause, requiring the solver to apply different prefixes to different nouns and maintain correct noun-modifier order throughout.

9. **Verify disambiguation and solvability.**
   - Confirm that no example allows the possessive prefix to be reanalyzed as a separate word or as a marker on the possessor. The prefix must be orthographically fused to the noun stem in every instance.
   - Confirm that the noun stem is uniquely identifiable across all possessive forms (the invariant portion must be the same string in every form of the same noun).
   - Confirm that the plural suffix is never ambiguous with the possessive prefix (they must differ in position AND in their distributional behavior: the prefix varies by possessor, the suffix is invariant across possessors).
   - Confirm that postpositions are never ambiguous with adjectives (use semantically distinct postpositions, or place them in contexts where their function is clear).
   - Confirm that every rule required by the questions is uniquely determined by at least one example in the data.
   - Confirm that the two core features (head-marking possessives, noun-before-modifier order) are each tested in at least three questions and are always tested in combination with at least one other rule.
   - Confirm that the puzzle does not require any prior knowledge of Abkhaz phonology, orthography, or grammar — all patterns must be inferable from the distributional data alone.
