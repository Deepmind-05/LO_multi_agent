

TARGET-LANGUAGE: Supyire (Senufo, Niger-Congo) (sup)

CONSTRUCTION RATIONALE

1. **Confirm the typological profile and identify the central inferential challenge.**
   Supyire exhibits head marking in possessive NPs (the possessed noun carries the possessive marker; the possessor is unmarked), a mixed modifier order (demonstratives and genitives precede the noun; adjectives and numerals follow it), an obligatory class-specific plural suffix, a class-specific definite article suffix, and a noun class system with at least 5 classes. The central inferential challenge for the solver is the noun class system: it is not directly labeled, and the solver must discover it by noticing that multiple agreement slots (plural, definite, possessive) show parallel class-specific variation across nouns. The possessive head-marking and mixed modifier order are secondary inferences that depend on correctly parsing the noun phrase, which in turn depends on identifying the noun class.

2. **Design the noun class system (3–4 classes for the puzzle).**
   Select 3 or 4 noun classes. Assign each class a distinct set of agreement forms across three slots: (a) plural suffix, (b) definite article suffix, (c) possessive marker suffix. The forms must be morphologically distinct from one another within each slot (so the solver can segment them) and distinct across slots for the same class (so the solver can tell which suffix is which). For example, if Class 1 has plural -ba, definite -na, possessive -ta, then no other class should share any of these forms in the same slot, and no slot should reuse a form from another slot for the same class. The classes should be semantically motivated where possible (e.g., humans, animals, plants, abstract/other) to give the solver a partial semantic cue, but at least one class should be "default" or formally assigned to prevent the solver from reducing the system to pure semantics.

3. **Select the vocabulary set.**
   Choose 8–12 countable nouns distributed across the 3–4 classes (at least 2–3 nouns per class). Include nouns from at least three semantic domains (e.g., kinship, animals, natural objects, body parts) so the solver can test whether class assignment is semantic or formal. Choose 4–6 adjectives that can modify nouns across classes. Choose 2–3 demonstratives (e.g., proximal, distal). Choose 3–5 verbs with person/number agreement. Include 2–3 proper nouns (personal names) to serve as unmarked possessors. Include 2–3 numerals (e.g., "two," "three") to demonstrate the noun-numeral order.

4. **Design the possessive head-marking.**
   In a possessive NP, the structure is: [Possessor-Noun] [Possessed-Noun + possessive-suffix + (definite-suffix) + (plural-suffix)]. The possessive suffix is a general marker (not person/number specific, per WALS 57A-4) but is class-specific (different form per noun class). The possessor noun appears in its bare form (no genitive case marking, no possessive affix). This is the critical contrast with English: the solver must notice that the possessor never changes form, while the possessed noun acquires an extra suffix. The possessive suffix must be morphologically distinct from the definite and plural suffixes to allow unambiguous segmentation.

5. **Design the definite article and plural suffix.**
   Both are suffixes on the noun, both are class-specific. The definite article marks a specific/known referent. The plural suffix is obligatory (every noun in the data appears with it, even in singular contexts—this reflects the typological fact that Supyire requires explicit plural marking, and in the puzzle, the "unmarked" form is actually the plural form, or alternatively, the puzzle can present both singular and plural forms with the plural being the marked one; the key is that the suffix is always present in the data). The order of suffixes on the noun should be fixed (e.g., root + possessive + definite + plural, or root + plural + definite + possessive—choose one and be consistent). This fixed order is itself a discoverable rule.

6. **Design the mixed modifier order.**
   Demonstratives and genitive (possessor) phrases precede the noun. Adjectives and numerals follow the noun. This creates a "sandwich" structure in complex NPs: [Demonstrative] [Possessor] [Noun + suffixes] [Adjective] [Numeral]. The solver must discover that the order is not uniform: some modifiers go before, some after. The critical evidence is a contrast: a sentence with a demonstrative + noun (showing demonstrative before) and a sentence with a noun + adjective (showing adjective after), ideally with the same noun in both to control for class effects.

7. **Design verb morphology and clause structure.**
   Use SOV word order. Verbs carry person/number agreement (prefix or suffix—choose one and be consistent). Include at least one aspectual marker (e.g., a suffix for progressive or habitual) that is morphologically distinct from person/number marking. Use postpositions for spatial/relational phrases (noun + postposition), consistent with the SOV profile. This provides confirmatory typological evidence: the solver who has already inferred noun-before-adjective and SOV will find the postpositional strategy consistent and reinforcing.

8. **Design secondary systems (negation, conjunctions, conditionals).**
   Include a preverbal negation particle. Include at least one conjunction (adversative or additive). Optionally include a conditional construction (if-then markers). These are not the focus of the puzzle but provide material for the harder translation questions and make the data set feel like a natural text rather than a grammar sketch.

9. **Construct baseline sentences (sentences 1–4): introduce the noun class system and obligatory plural.**
   - Sentence 1: A simple declarative sentence with a single noun (Class A) in the definite + plural form, as subject of a verb. This introduces the noun with its class-specific suffixes and the basic SOV order.
   - Sentence 2: A sentence with a noun from Class B in the same structural position. The solver now sees that the suffixes differ from Sentence 1, raising the question: why?
   - Sentence 3: A sentence with a noun from Class C (or D) in the same position. Now three (or four) different suffix sets are visible. The solver should begin grouping nouns by their suffix patterns.
   - Sentence 4: A sentence with a second noun from Class A (a different noun, same class). This confirms that the suffixes are not idiosyncratic to a single noun but are shared by a group of nouns—i.e., they are class markers.

   At this stage, the solver should be able to: (a) identify that nouns carry suffixes, (b) notice that the suffixes vary across nouns in a systematic way, (c) group nouns into classes based on shared suffix forms, and (d) infer that the suffixes are obligatory (they appear on every noun in every sentence).

10. **Construct contrastive sentences (sentences 5–8): introduce possessive head-marking.**
    - Sentence 5: A possessive NP where a proper noun is the possessor and a Class A noun is the possessed. The possessed noun carries the Class A possessive suffix. The proper noun is bare. This is the first evidence of head marking.
    - Sentence 6: A possessive NP with the same proper noun as possessor but a Class B noun as possessed. The possessed noun now carries the Class B possessive suffix. The proper noun is still bare. This confirms: (a) the possessor is unmarked, (b) the possessive marker is on the head, (c) the possessive marker is class-specific.
    - Sentence 7: A non-possessive NP with the same Class A noun from Sentence 5, now without the possessive suffix. This provides a minimal pair: with vs. without the possessive marker, confirming that the marker is specifically a possessive marker (not just a general suffix that appears in all contexts).
    - Sentence 8: A possessive NP where the possessor is a common noun (not a proper name), e.g., "the child's house." The possessor noun is bare (no genitive case, no possessive affix). The possessed noun carries the possessive suffix. This confirms that the head-marking strategy applies regardless of possessor type.

    At this stage, the solver should infer: the possessed noun (head) is marked for possession; the possessor is unmarked; the possessive marker is class-specific; the possessive marker is distinct from the definite and plural suffixes.

11. **Construct modifier-order sentences (sentences 9–12): introduce the mixed order.**
    - Sentence 9: A noun phrase with a demonstrative + noun (e.g., "this house"). The demonstrative precedes the noun. The noun carries its class-specific suffixes.
    - Sentence 10: A noun phrase with a noun + adjective (e.g., "house big"). The adjective follows the noun. The noun carries its class-specific suffixes.
    - Sentence 11: A noun phrase with a noun + numeral (e.g., "house two"). The numeral follows the noun. This confirms that the "after" position is not limited to adjectives.
    - Sentence 12: A complex NP combining a demonstrative + possessor + noun + adjective (e.g., "this [person's] house big"). This shows the full sandwich structure and confirms that the order is: [Demonstrative] [Possessor] [Noun+suffixes] [Adjective]. The solver must now integrate the possessive head-marking (from step 10) with the modifier order (from steps 9–11).

    At this stage, the solver should infer: modifier order is not uniform; demonstratives and possessors precede the noun; adjectives and numerals follow the noun; the head noun (with its class-specific morphology) is the anchor point for determining which side each modifier is on.

12. **Construct secondary-system sentences (sentences 13–16): introduce aspect, negation, conjunctions.**
    - Sentence 13: A verb with the aspectual marker in a context where the interpretation is unambiguous (e.g., a progressive reading).
    - Sentence 14: A negated clause, showing the negation particle in its canonical position.
    - Sentence 15: Two clauses linked by a conjunction (adversative or additive).
    - Sentence 16: A sentence combining a postpositional phrase with the SOV order, confirming the postpositional strategy.

    These sentences are not the focus of the core inferences but provide material for the harder translation questions and make the data set feel like a coherent text.

13. **Construct complex sentences (sentences 17–20): combine three or more systems simultaneously.**
    - Sentence 17: A possessive NP (head-marked, class-specific) + adjective modifier (after the noun) + definite article + plural suffix, all on the same noun, in an SOV clause with person/number agreement on the verb.
    - Sentence 18: A demonstrative + possessive NP + numeral, showing the full modifier sandwich, in a clause with the aspectual marker.
    - Sentence 19: A negated clause containing a possessive NP with a Class C or D noun (a class the solver has seen less frequently), testing whether the solver can generalize the class-specific forms to less-frequent classes.
    - Sentence 20: The most complex sentence in the set, combining at least four systems (e.g., possessive head-marking + mixed modifier order + aspect + conjunction + postpositional phrase). This sentence should be translatable only if the solver has correctly identified all the core rules.

14. **Design the translation questions with progressive difficulty.**
    - Q1 (easy, target→English): A simple sentence with a single noun (Class A) in definite + plural form, as subject of a verb. Tests: noun class identification (the solver must recognize the Class A suffixes), obligatory plural, SOV order.
    - Q2 (easy, target→English): A possessive NP with a proper noun possessor and a Class B possessed noun. Tests: head-marking inference (the solver must identify that the suffix on the possessed noun is the possessive marker, not the definite or plural), class-specific possessive form.
    - Q3 (medium, target→English): A sentence with a demonstrative + noun + adjective, where the noun is Class C. Tests: mixed modifier order (demonstrative before, adjective after), class-specific suffixes on a less-frequent class.
    - Q4 (medium, target→English): A sentence with a possessive NP + adjective modifier + numeral, all on the same noun (Class D). Tests: full modifier sandwich, head-marking, class-specific forms, integration of multiple rules.
    - Q5 (medium-hard, English→target): A sentence requiring the solver to produce a possessive NP with a specific noun class, a definite article, and an adjective modifier. The solver must select the correct class-specific forms for all three suffixes and place the adjective in the correct position.
    - Q6 (hard, English→target): A sentence requiring a demonstrative + possessive NP + adjective + numeral, with the aspectual marker on the verb. The solver must produce the full modifier sandwich with correct class-specific forms and correct verb morphology.
    - Q7 (hard, English→target): A sentence requiring a possessive NP (with a common noun possessor) + negation + conjunction + postpositional phrase. The solver must leave the possessor unmarked, apply the correct possessive suffix to the head, position the negator, select the conjunction, and use the postposition.
    - Q8 (hard, English→target): The most complex translation, requiring at least four systems simultaneously (e.g., possessive head-marking + mixed modifier order + aspect + negation + class-specific morphology on a less-frequent class).

15. **Verify disambiguation and solvability.**
    - Confirm that no sentence allows an alternative analysis where the possessive marker could be interpreted as a definite article or plural suffix. The three suffix types must be morphologically distinct in every class, and the data must include at least one minimal pair (same noun, with vs. without the possessive marker) to confirm the possessive marker's identity.
    - Confirm that no sentence allows an alternative analysis where the modifier order is uniform. The data must include at least one demonstrative-before-noun example and at least one adjective-after-noun example, ideally with the same noun to control for class effects.
    - Confirm that the noun class system is uniquely determined: no two classes should share a suffix form in any slot, and the data should include enough nouns per class (at least 2–3) to rule out the possibility that the suffixes are idiosyncratic to individual nouns.
    - Confirm that the possessive head-marking is unambiguously head-marking (not dependent marking): in every possessive NP, the possessor must be morphologically bare, and the possessed noun must carry the marker. No sentence should show a possessor with any kind of possessive affix.
    - Confirm that every rule required by the questions is uniquely determined by at least one sentence in the data. No question should require a rule that is absent from the data.
    - Confirm that the noun class system is tested in at least 4 of the 8 questions (since it underlies all the other inferences), head-marking in at least 3, and mixed modifier order in at least 3. Each core feature should always be tested in combination with at least one other rule.
    - Confirm that tone is not used as a distinguishing feature anywhere in the data. All class distinctions and morphological contrasts should be segmental (vowel/consonant differences in suffixes), not tonal.
