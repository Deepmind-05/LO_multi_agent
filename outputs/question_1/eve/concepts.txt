

TARGET-LANGUAGE: Evenki (Tungusic, Altaic)

CORE

1. Concept: Double marking in possessive noun phrases (adapted from source 81A "Dependent marking")
   - Target-language realization: Evenki exhibits double marking in possessive constructions (WALS 24A-3). The possessed noun takes a possessive suffix (WALS 57A-2), and the possessor is marked with a genitive case suffix. Both elements in the possessive phrase carry overt morphological marking.
   - Intended solver inference: From multiple possessive examples, the solver identifies that BOTH the possessor and the possessed noun carry marking, distinguishing this from a single-marking strategy. The solver can contrast this with what they might expect (one element marked, the other unmarked).
   - Priority: REQUIRED

2. Concept: Modifier-before-noun order (adapted from source 82A "Noun before modifier")
   - Target-language realization: Evenki consistently places modifiers before the head noun: adjectives precede nouns (WALS 87A-1), demonstratives precede nouns (88A-1), numerals precede nouns (89A-1), relative clauses precede nouns (90A-2). This is the opposite of the source language's pattern.
   - Intended solver inference: From multiple noun-phrase examples, the solver identifies a consistent pre-nominal modifier order across different modifier types (adjectival, demonstrative, numeral). The consistency across types strengthens the inference.
   - Priority: HIGH

TARGET-SPECIFIC

1. Phenomenon: Obligatory double negation
   - Intended solver inference: From negative sentence examples, the solver discovers that negation requires TWO morphological elements: a preverbal negative word AND a negative suffix on the verb (WALS 143A-14, 144N-4: SONeg[V-Neg]). Neither element alone is sufficient. The solver can identify this as a "double" strategy parallel to the possessive double marking.
   - Interaction with CORE concepts: Creates a typological parallel with double marking in possession — both domains in Evenki employ a "doubling" strategy. This gives the puzzle a unifying thematic insight (Evenki as a language that "doubles up" in certain domains).
   - Difficulty contribution: HIGH. The solver must notice that two separate elements are both obligatory, not that one is optional or that one is the "real" negation and the other is something else. This is a non-trivial inference.
   - Priority: HIGH

2. Phenomenon: Suffixing as the primary morphological strategy
   - Intended solver inference: Across all domains (case, possession, negation, number), Evenki marks grammatical relations via suffixes on the relevant word (WALS 26A-2: strongly suffixing; 51A-1: case suffixes; 57A-2: possessive suffixes; 143F-2: negative suffix). The solver can identify a consistent suffixing strategy as the language's default morphological means.
   - Interaction with CORE concepts: The suffixing strategy is the mechanism that realizes both the double marking in possession (possessive suffix + genitive suffix) and the double negation (negative suffix + preverbal word). It provides the morphological "glue" that makes the other patterns work.
   - Difficulty contribution: MEDIUM. It's a unifying observation that helps the solver organize their understanding, but it's relatively straightforward once the individual patterns are identified.
   - Priority: HIGH

3. Phenomenon: Rich case system (10+ cases)
   - Intended solver inference: If the puzzle includes examples with different case forms on nouns, the solver can infer that Evenki has a large number of distinct case categories (WALS 49A-8). The solver may not be able to name all cases, but can recognize that there are many distinct suffixes marking different grammatical/semantic roles.
   - Interaction with CORE concepts: The genitive case (used in possessive constructions) is one member of a larger case system. Showing multiple cases provides context for why the possessor in a possessive phrase is "marked" — it's receiving a case suffix that is part of a broader system.
   - Difficulty contribution: MEDIUM-HIGH. Adds complexity because the solver must distinguish case suffixes from possessive suffixes and other suffixes. However, if too many cases are shown, the puzzle becomes unwieldy.
   - Priority: OPTIONAL

INTERACTIONS

1. Double marking (possession) × Double negation:
   - Both domains in Evenki employ a "doubling" strategy where two morphological elements are required. This creates a cross-domain parallel that gives the puzzle a thematic coherence. The solver who identifies both patterns can make a higher-level observation about the language's typological profile.

2. Suffixing strategy × Double marking (possession):
   - The suffixing strategy explains HOW double marking is realized: the possessed noun gets a possessive suffix, the possessor gets a genitive case suffix. Without the suffixing strategy, the double marking would be harder to parse. The solver can use the consistent suffixing to segment words into stem + suffix(es).

3. Suffixing strategy × Double negation:
   - The negative suffix on the verb is one of the two required negation elements. The solver must identify that the verb carries a suffix that is part of the negation, not a separate grammatical category (e.g., tense or aspect). This requires the solver to compare affirmative and negative verb forms.

4. Modifier-before-noun order × Suffixing:
   - Creates a structural contrast: modifiers are PRE-nominal (free words before the noun), while inflectional marking is SUFFIXAL (attached to the right edge of the noun). This contrast is typologically interesting and helps the solver distinguish between free modifiers and bound morphological markers.

CONSTRAINTS

1. The puzzle must provide enough possessive examples (at least 3-4 with different persons/numbers) for the solver to reliably identify double marking rather than attributing one of the two markers to a different function.

2. The double negation examples must include at least one affirmative counterpart to allow the solver to isolate the negative suffix by comparison. Without an affirmative form, the solver cannot determine which suffix is "negative" vs. some other category.

3. If the rich case system is included, the puzzle must limit the number of distinct case forms shown to avoid overwhelming the solver. The genitive (for possession) should be clearly identifiable; other cases can be shown but need not all be distinguished by the solver.

4. The modifier-before-noun order should be demonstrated across at least two different modifier types (e.g., adjective + noun, and numeral + noun) to establish that it is a general pattern, not specific to one modifier class.

5. All morphological marking should be suffixal (no prefixing or infixing) to maintain consistency with Evenki's strongly suffixing profile and to keep the puzzle's morphological analysis tractable.

6. The puzzle should avoid using obligatorily possessed nouns (WALS 58A-1) in the data, as these would complicate the possessive analysis by introducing a class of nouns that cannot appear without a possessor — a constraint the solver would have no way to infer from limited data.
