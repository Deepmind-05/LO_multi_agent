

TARGET-LANGUAGE: Evenki (eve)

CONSTRUCTION RATIONALE

1. **Establish the morphological baseline: suffixal noun structure.** Begin by defining the noun's internal structure for the puzzle. Every noun in the data must carry at minimum a plural suffix and a case suffix (nominative in subject/object position). The suffixal chain on nouns is fixed: root + plural + possessive (if applicable) + case. This means even a bare singular noun in subject position will show a plural suffix (since plural marking is obligatory in Evenki) and a nominative case suffix. The solver's first discovery should be that nouns are not bare roots but always carry suffixes, and that these suffixes appear in a consistent order. Construct 2–3 simple intransitive sentences (e.g., "the dog runs," "the children sleep") where the nouns appear in subject position. The solver should be able to segment the noun into root + plural + case and recognize the suffixing tendency. This step is foundational: all later morphological analysis (possessive, case distinctions) depends on the solver having identified the suffixal structure.

2. **Introduce the possessive system with double marking.** Construct 3–4 possessive noun phrases using different possessor types (e.g., a 3rd-person singular pronoun, a 2nd-person singular pronoun, a proper noun, a 1st-person singular pronoun). In each case, the possessor must carry a genitive case suffix, and the possessed noun must carry a possessive suffix that agrees with the possessor's person/number. The possessed noun also carries its obligatory plural and case suffixes. The critical observable contrast is: the possessor is NOT bare—it carries a case suffix (genitive), and the possessed noun is NOT bare either—it carries a possessive suffix in addition to its plural and case suffixes. The solver must discover that BOTH elements are marked. To force this inference, include at least one pair where the same possessed noun appears with two different possessors, showing that the possessive suffix on the possessed noun changes (agrees with possessor) while the genitive suffix on the possessor remains constant (it is a case marker, not a possessive agreement marker). The solver should infer: (a) the genitive suffix on the possessor is a case form (it will reappear in non-possessive contexts later); (b) the possessive suffix on the possessed noun is an agreement marker that encodes the possessor's person/number; (c) both are obligatory in possessive constructions.

3. **Establish modifier-before-head word order across multiple modifier types.** Construct sentences showing at least three distinct modifier types preceding their head noun: (a) an adjective modifying a noun (e.g., "big dog"), (b) a genitive NP modifying a noun (e.g., "the dog's owner"—here the genitive NP is the modifier and the head noun is "owner"), (c) a demonstrative or numeral preceding a noun (e.g., "this house," "three children"). In each case, the modifier appears before the head noun. The genitive NP in (b) simultaneously reinforces the possessive system from Step 2 (the genitive suffix is visible) and the word order pattern. The solver should infer a systematic modifier-before-head order that is not limited to one modifier type. This is a REQUIRED core concept and must be established with sufficient breadth to rule out the hypothesis that only one type of modifier precedes the noun.

4. **Introduce the broader case system.** Now that the solver has identified the genitive suffix from possessive data (Step 2), introduce 3–4 additional case forms in non-possessive contexts. For example: a locative case ("in the house"), an allative case ("to the house"), an ablative case ("from the house"), and a dative case ("to the person"). Each case must have a distinct suffix form (no syncretism). The solver should recognize that the genitive suffix seen in Step 2 is one member of a larger paradigm of case suffixes, all of which are distinct from one another. The no-syncretism property means the solver can identify each case purely from its form without ambiguity. This step also reinforces the suffixing tendency: all case marking is suffixal. The interaction with Step 2 is critical: the solver should be able to look at a genitive-marked NP in a non-possessive context (e.g., "from the house" where "house" is genitive) and recognize the same suffix that appeared on the possessor in possessive constructions.

5. **Establish SOV word order and introduce the double negation system.** Construct 2–3 affirmative transitive sentences that clearly show SOV order (subject, object, verb). The object should carry an accusative or other oblique case suffix (from the case system in Step 4), which helps the solver identify argument roles. Then construct 2–3 negative sentences. In each negative sentence, BOTH a preverbal negative word AND a negative suffix on the verb must appear. The preverbal negative word appears between the object and the verb (consistent with SOV). The negative suffix attaches to the verb stem. To establish obligatoriness, include at least two negative sentences with different verbs and different subjects, showing that both elements are always present. The solver should infer: (a) the basic word order is SOV; (b) negation requires two separate elements—a free preverbal word and a bound verbal suffix; (c) both are obligatory (neither can be omitted). The preverbal negative word's position provides independent confirmation of SOV order, creating a cross-check with the word order evidence from affirmative sentences.

6. **Introduce the inclusive/exclusive distinction (if included).** Construct 2–3 sentences where a 1st-person plural pronoun appears as a possessor in a possessive construction. Use context to make the inclusive vs. exclusive distinction inferable: for example, a sentence where the speaker addresses someone directly and says "our house" (inclusive, including the addressee) versus a sentence where the speaker refers to "our house" in a context where the addressee is not included (exclusive). The two 1st-person plural forms must be morphologically distinct. The solver should infer that Evenki distinguishes inclusive and exclusive 1st-person plural, and that this distinction is relevant to the possessive suffix (the possessive suffix on the possessed noun must agree with the correct 1st-person plural form). This adds a semantic inference layer to the possessive system.

7. **Construct contrastive and disambiguating evidence.** At this point, the solver should have identified: suffixal morphology, double possessive marking, modifier-before-head order, a multi-case system, SOV order, and double negation. Now construct targeted contrasts to eliminate competing hypotheses:
   - A contrast showing that the genitive suffix on a possessor is the SAME form as the genitive suffix in a non-possessive genitive NP (e.g., "the house's roof" vs. "from the house"). This confirms the genitive is a case marker, not a dedicated possessive marker.
   - A contrast showing that the possessive suffix on the possessed noun changes with the possessor's person/number (e.g., "my house" vs. "your house" vs. "his house"), confirming it is an agreement marker.
   - A contrast showing that the modifier-before-head order holds even when the modifier is a complex NP (e.g., a genitive NP modifying a noun that is itself modified by an adjective: "the big dog's owner" → [big dog-Gen] owner). This tests whether the solver generalizes the word order pattern to nested structures.
   - A contrast showing that the negative word and negative suffix both appear even in a sentence with a complex verb (e.g., a verb with aspectual or evidential marking), confirming that the negative suffix attaches to the verb stem regardless of other verbal morphology.

8. **Construct maximally complex sentences combining multiple systems.** Create 3–4 sentences that simultaneously instantiate: possessive double marking + modifier-before-head order + a non-nominative case on the possessed noun + SOV word order + double negation. For example, a sentence meaning something like "The big dog's owner did not see the three children in the house." This requires: a genitive NP (modifier) before the head noun "owner," the head noun carrying a possessive suffix agreeing with the genitive possessor, the object "children" carrying a numeral modifier before it and an appropriate case suffix, the locative "in the house" showing a case form, the preverbal negative word between object and verb, and the negative suffix on the verb. The solver must integrate all discovered rules simultaneously.

9. **Design the question set with progressive difficulty.** Construct 6–8 translation questions (mix of target-to-English and English-to-target) with the following structure:
   - **Q1 (easy, target-to-English):** A simple affirmative sentence testing basic SOV word order + noun suffixal structure (plural + case). All elements appear individually in the data. The solver must segment the noun and identify the case.
   - **Q2 (easy, target-to-English):** A sentence with a possessive NP, testing double marking. The solver must identify both the genitive on the possessor and the possessive suffix on the possessed noun, and recognize that both are required.
   - **Q3 (medium, target-to-English):** A sentence with a modifier (adjective or demonstrative) before a head noun that is also possessed. Tests modifier-before-head order + possessive double marking in combination. The modifier's position relative to the possessive NP structure must be correctly identified.
   - **Q4 (medium, target-to-English):** A negative sentence testing double negation + SOV word order. The solver must identify both the preverbal negative word and the verbal negative suffix, and correctly parse the word order.
   - **Q5 (medium-hard, English-to-target):** A sentence requiring the solver to produce a possessive NP with a specific possessor (testing correct possessive suffix selection) and a non-nominative case on the possessed noun (testing case selection from the paradigm). The solver must apply the suffixal chain in the correct order.
   - **Q6 (medium-hard, English-to-target):** A sentence requiring modifier-before-head order + a genitive modifier + a non-nominative case on the head noun + SOV word order. The solver must correctly order the modifier before the head, apply the genitive to the modifier, apply the correct case to the head, and place the verb at the end.
   - **Q7 (hard, English-to-target):** A negative sentence with a possessive NP (where the possessor is a 1st-person plural pronoun, testing inclusive/exclusive if included) + a modifier on the possessed noun + a non-nominative case. The solver must apply double negation, double possessive marking, modifier-before-head order, case selection, and (if included) the correct 1st-person plural form.
   - **Q8 (hard, English-to-target):** The most complex sentence, combining at least four systems simultaneously (e.g., possessive double marking + modifier-before-head + double negation + a non-nominative case + SOV). The solver must integrate all rules in a configuration not seen verbatim in the data.

10. **Verify solvability and disambiguation.** Before finalizing the puzzle, check the following:
    - Confirm that no sentence in the data allows the solver to interpret the possessive system as single-marking (either pure dependent or pure head marking). The double marking must be unambiguous: in every possessive NP, both the possessor's genitive suffix and the possessed noun's possessive suffix must be visible and segmentable.
    - Confirm that no sentence allows the modifier to be interpreted as following the head noun. In every modifier-head sequence, the head must be unambiguously identifiable (e.g., by its ability to take a possessive suffix, or by its semantic category, or by its case marking).
    - Confirm that the case system is unambiguous: no two case suffixes share the same form, so the solver can identify each case from form alone without semantic guessing.
    - Confirm that the double negation is unambiguous: in every negative sentence, both the preverbal word and the verbal suffix are present, and neither can be reanalyzed as part of another morphological process.
    - Confirm that the suffixal chain order (root + plural + possessive + case) is consistent across all examples, so the solver can use it as a segmentation guide for complex noun forms.
    - Confirm that every rule tested in the questions is uniquely determined by at least one sentence in the data. No question should require a rule that is absent from the evidence.
    - Confirm that the two core concepts (double possessive marking and modifier-before-head order) are each tested in at least three questions and are always tested in combination with at least one other rule, ensuring the solver must integrate multiple inferred rules simultaneously.
    - Confirm that the puzzle does not rely on any prior knowledge of Evenki, Tungusic languages, or Altaic languages. All properties must be inferable from the presented data alone.
