

TARGET-LANGUAGE: Evenki (eve)

CONSTRUCTION RATIONALE

1. **Establish the morphological baseline: suffixing as the default strategy.**
   Begin by selecting a controlled vocabulary set of 6–8 countable nouns spanning at least two semantic domains (e.g., kinship terms like "child, mother, elder" and natural/cultural objects like "river, fire, reindeer, tent"). Select 4–6 adjectives (e.g., "big, cold, old, new, white") and 4–6 dynamic verbs (e.g., "go, see, eat, fall, build"). Select 2–3 proper nouns (personal names) to serve as possessor referents. The person/number paradigm should include at minimum 1sg, 2sg, 3sg, 1pl, 2pl, 3pl.

   All grammatical marking in the data must be realized as suffixes on the relevant word. This is not a rule the solver must "discover" as a separate item — rather, it is the consistent morphological substrate that makes all other patterns parseable. The solver will implicitly absorb the suffixing strategy as they segment words into stem + suffix(es). Ensure that no prefixing, infixing, or suppletion appears anywhere in the data.

2. **Introduce modifier-before-noun order in isolation (Stage 1: simple observation).**
   Construct 2–3 baseline sentences that each contain a noun modified by a different type of modifier:
   - One sentence with an adjective modifying a noun (e.g., "big river" or "cold fire").
   - One sentence with a numeral or demonstrative modifying a noun (e.g., "two reindeer" or "that tent").
   - Optionally, one sentence with a relative clause modifying a noun.

   In each case, the modifier must precede the head noun. The head noun should be unambiguously identifiable (e.g., by its ability to take a possessive suffix in a later sentence, or by its semantic category). The purpose of showing at least two modifier types is to let the solver infer a GENERAL pre-nominal modifier order rather than a class-specific rule. These sentences should be simple (SVO or SOV with a single clause, no possession, no negation) so the modifier-noun sequence is the only structural feature to attend to.

3. **Introduce possessive constructions with double marking (Stage 2: core discovery).**
   Construct 3–4 possessive sentences that establish the double-marking pattern. Each possessive noun phrase must show:
   - The possessor bearing a genitive case suffix (marking it as the possessor).
   - The possessed noun bearing a possessive suffix that encodes the possessor's person and/or number.

   Critical design choices:
   - Use at least two different possessor persons/numbers across the set (e.g., 1sg possessor in one sentence, 3sg possessor in another, 2pl possessor in a third). This allows the solver to see that the possessive suffix on the possessed noun CHANGES to reflect the possessor's features, confirming it is a dependent marker.
   - Include at least one sentence where the possessor is a proper noun (a personal name). The proper noun should still receive a genitive suffix (confirming that even "unmarked" in the sense of not being a pronoun, it is still case-marked). This prevents the solver from hypothesizing that only pronouns are marked.
   - The possessed nouns should vary across sentences (different nouns, different semantic categories) to show the possessive suffix is a general property of nouns, not specific to one lexical item.

   The solver's intended inference: "Both elements in a possessive phrase carry marking. The possessor gets a case suffix; the possessed noun gets a possessive suffix that agrees with the possessor." This is the key contrast with a single-marking strategy — the solver should notice that NEITHER element is bare.

4. **Provide contrastive evidence to force unique induction of double marking (Stage 3: disambiguation).**
   Construct 2–3 sentences that specifically rule out alternative analyses:
   - A near-minimal pair: two sentences with the same possessed noun but different possessor persons (e.g., "my house" vs. "your house" vs. "their house"). The possessive suffix on the possessed noun must differ across these, while the genitive suffix on the possessor also differs. This confirms that the possessive suffix is DEPENDENT (it encodes the possessor's features) and that the genitive is a separate, independent marker on the possessor.
   - A sentence where the possessed noun is modified by an adjective AND possessed simultaneously (e.g., "my big house" or "their old tent"). This shows that the modifier precedes the possessed noun, and the possessed noun carries both its possessive suffix and (implicitly) its ability to be modified. The structure should be: [modifier] [possessed-noun + possessive-suffix] [possessor + genitive-suffix] (or the Evenki-appropriate linear order). This tests the interaction between modifier order and possessive marking.
   - Optionally: a sentence where the possessor is itself modified (e.g., "the old man's house"), showing that the genitive suffix attaches to the end of the entire possessor phrase (after any modifier), reinforcing the suffixing strategy.

5. **Introduce double negation (Stage 4: target-specific discovery).**
   Construct 2–3 negative sentences and 1–2 affirmative counterparts:
   - Each negative sentence must contain BOTH a preverbal negative word (a free morpheme positioned before the verb) AND a negative suffix on the verb itself. Neither element alone should appear.
   - The affirmative counterpart(s) must use the same verb (or a closely related verb form) WITHOUT the negative suffix, allowing the solver to isolate the negative suffix by direct comparison. For example, if the affirmative verb is "go-PAST-3sg" and the negative is "NEG go-PAST-NEG-3sg", the solver can see that the negative suffix is an additional element not present in the affirmative.
   - Use at least two different verbs in the negative examples to show the negative suffix is a general verbal category, not verb-specific.
   - The preverbal negative word should be consistent across all negative sentences (same form, same position).

   The solver's intended inference: "Negation requires two elements: a preverbal word AND a suffix on the verb. Removing either one would be ungrammatical (implied by the fact that both always co-occur)." The parallel with double marking in possession should be discoverable: both domains in this language employ a "doubling" strategy.

6. **Introduce the case system in a limited way (Stage 5: contextual enrichment).**
   If including the rich case system (optional but recommended for depth), show 3–5 different case suffixes on nouns across the data set. The genitive (already seen in possessive constructions) should be the most prominent. Other cases (e.g., dative, locative, accusative) can appear in simple sentences:
   - "I go to the river" (allative or dative on "river")
   - "The child is at the fire" (locative on "fire")
   - "I see the reindeer" (accusative on "reindeer")

   The solver need not identify all cases by name, but should recognize that nouns can take different suffixes marking different grammatical/semantic roles. This provides context for why the genitive in possessive constructions is "just another case" — it is part of a larger system. Limit the number of distinct case forms to avoid overwhelming the solver; 3–5 total (including genitive) is sufficient.

7. **Construct maximally complex sentences combining multiple systems (Stage 6: integration).**
   Construct 3–4 sentences that combine three or more systems simultaneously:
   - A sentence combining possession + modification + negation (e.g., "I don't see their big tent" — possessive double marking + adjective before noun + double negation).
   - A sentence combining possession + modification + a non-genitive case on the possessed noun (e.g., "I go to my old house" — possessive marking + modifier order + allative/locative case).
   - A sentence combining double negation + a case-marked object + person/number agreement (e.g., "They don't eat the cold reindeer" — double negation + accusative on object + 3pl agreement).
   - The most complex sentence: combine at least four systems (e.g., possession + modification + negation + case + person marking).

   These sentences should use vocabulary and structures already seen in isolation, so the solver can decompose them using previously inferred rules. No entirely new morphological element should appear for the first time in a complex sentence.

8. **Design translation questions with progressive difficulty.**
   Create 6–8 questions of increasing difficulty. Each question must test at least two inferred rules simultaneously (never a single rule in isolation). The progression should be:

   - **Q1 (easy, target→English):** A simple sentence testing modifier-before-noun order + person/number marking. All elements appear verbatim in the data. The solver only needs to recognize the modifier-noun order and the agreement suffix.
   - **Q2 (easy, target→English):** A sentence testing double possessive marking + a non-genitive case. The solver must identify both the genitive on the possessor and the possessive suffix on the possessed noun, plus recognize a different case suffix.
   - **Q3 (medium, target→English):** A sentence testing double negation + modifier order + person marking. The solver must identify both negation elements, the pre-nominal modifier, and the agreement.
   - **Q4 (medium, target→English):** A sentence combining possession + modification + a case other than genitive (e.g., "I go to their big river"). Tests the interaction of all three systems.
   - **Q5 (medium-hard, English→target):** A sentence requiring the solver to PRODUCE double possessive marking (select the correct genitive suffix on the possessor AND the correct possessive suffix on the possessed noun) + place a modifier before the possessed noun. The solver must apply two rules in the correct morphological positions.
   - **Q6 (hard, English→target):** A sentence requiring double negation + possession + modification. The solver must produce the preverbal negative word, the negative suffix on the verb, the genitive on the possessor, the possessive suffix on the possessed noun, and place the modifier before the possessed noun. Five distinct operations in one sentence.
   - **Q7 (hard, English→target):** A sentence requiring a proper-noun possessor + modification + a non-genitive case + person marking. The solver must apply the genitive to the proper noun, the possessive suffix to the possessed noun, a different case suffix (e.g., locative or allative), and place the modifier correctly.
   - **Q8 (hardest, English→target):** A sentence combining at least four systems: double negation + double possessive marking + modification + a non-genitive case + person/number agreement. This is the "capstone" question requiring full integration.

9. **Verify disambiguation and solvability.**
   - Confirm that no possessive sentence allows an analysis where only ONE element is marked. In every possessive NP, both the possessor (genitive suffix) and the possessed noun (possessive suffix) must be visibly marked. If any sentence could be reanalyzed as single-marking (e.g., if the genitive suffix were absent in one example), the double-marking inference would be undermined.
   - Confirm that no noun-modifier sequence allows a modifier-after-noun reading. In every instance, the modifier must unambiguously precede the noun. The head noun should be identifiable by its capacity to take suffixes (possessive, case) or by its semantic category.
   - Confirm that no negative sentence allows an analysis where only one negation element is present. Both the preverbal word and the verbal suffix must appear in every negative example. The affirmative counterpart must clearly lack the verbal suffix (while also lacking the preverbal word), confirming both are part of the negation strategy.
   - Confirm that the possessive suffix on the possessed noun is not confusable with a case suffix. The possessive suffix should be morphologically distinct from all case suffixes (different form, or at least different distribution — possessive suffixes only appear on possessed nouns, case suffixes appear on nouns in various grammatical roles).
   - Confirm that every rule required by the questions is uniquely determined by at least one sentence in the data. No question should require a rule that has no evidential basis in the provided sentences.
   - Confirm that the two core features (double possessive marking, modifier-before-noun order) are each tested in at least three of the questions, and that they are always tested in combination with at least one other rule.
   - Confirm that the double negation is tested in at least two questions (one target→English, one English→target) to ensure the solver can both recognize and produce the pattern.

10. **Thematic coherence check: the "doubling" insight.**
    The overall puzzle should allow a solver who identifies both double possessive marking and double negation to make a higher-level observation: this language employs a "doubling" strategy in multiple grammatical domains. This is not a rule the solver must state, but the puzzle's design should make this parallel discoverable and satisfying. The construction should ensure that the two doubling phenomena are structurally analogous (two elements, both obligatory, both morphologically realized) so the parallel is transparent once both are identified. This gives the puzzle a unifying aesthetic that rewards careful observation across domains.
