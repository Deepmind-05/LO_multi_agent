

TARGET-LANGUAGE: Lezgian (lez)

CONSTRUCTION RATIONALE

1. **Establish the morphological baseline: nouns, plural, and simple intransitive clauses.**
   Select a controlled vocabulary of 6–8 countable nouns spanning at least two semantic domains (e.g., kinship terms and natural objects). Include at least two nouns that will appear in both singular and plural forms across the data. Construct 2–3 simple intransitive clauses (S + V) using a small set of 3–4 intransitive verbs (e.g., "come," "sit," "fall," "die"). The verbs must be invariant—no person, number, or aspect morphology. The solver's first discovery is twofold: (a) nouns can bear a suffix that correlates with plurality (the plural suffix), and (b) verbs do not change form regardless of who the subject is. This establishes the morphological "ground floor": the solver learns to segment noun stems, identify the plural suffix as a fixed reference point, and note the absence of verbal agreement. This last point is critical groundwork—it means the solver will later be forced to look at noun morphology for role identification.

2. **Introduce possessive noun phrases to reveal dependent marking.**
   Construct 3–4 sentences containing possessive NPs. In each, the possessor is a noun (a proper name, a kinship term, or a common noun) and the possessed noun is a different noun. The possessor must bear a genitive suffix; the possessed noun must be morphologically bare (no possessive affix, no agreement marker). Vary the possessor across sentences (different nouns, at least one proper name, at least one common noun) while keeping the possessed noun constant in at least one pair. The solver's discovery: within the NP, exactly one element carries a suffix and the other does not. The solver must infer that the marked element is the possessor (the dependent) and the unmarked element is the possessed noun (the head). This is the core dependent-marking inference. The plural suffix from Stage 1 helps here: if a plural possessor appears, the solver can see plural suffix + genitive suffix stacking, confirming that the genitive is a separate, additional suffix on the same noun.

3. **Establish modifier-before-noun order with multiple modifier types.**
   Construct 3–4 sentences showing at least two different types of modifiers preceding their head noun. The most natural choices for Lezgian are: (a) adjectives modifying nouns, and (b) the genitive possessor itself (already established in Stage 2). Optionally include a demonstrative or numeral if the vocabulary allows. The critical design principle: the modifier must consistently precede the head noun in every instance. The solver's discovery: there is a consistent head-final ordering within noun phrases. The "aha!" interaction with Stage 2: the genitive possessor is simultaneously the marked element (dependent marking) AND the pre-noun element (modifier-before-noun). The solver should notice that the marked/dependent element systematically precedes the unmarked/head element. This unifies two seemingly separate observations into a single typological principle: marking and ordering are aligned in the same direction.

4. **Introduce transitive clauses to reveal ergative-absolutive alignment.**
   Construct 3–4 transitive clauses (A + P + V) using 2–3 transitive verbs (e.g., "see," "take," "kill," "eat"). The agent (A) must bear an ergative suffix; the patient (P) must be unmarked (absolutive). Crucially, include at least one transitive clause where the patient is the same noun that appeared as an intransitive subject in Stage 1, and at least one where the agent is the same noun that appeared as an intransitive subject. This creates a direct contrast: the same noun is unmarked when it is an intransitive subject (S) or a transitive patient (P), but bears a suffix when it is a transitive agent (A). The solver's discovery: there is a case contrast in transitive clauses that parallels the genitive contrast in possessive NPs. The "aha!" interaction with Stage 2: the language marks the "agentive" or "dependent" role (the agent in a transitive clause, the possessor in a possessive NP) with a case suffix, while leaving the "patientive" or "head" role (the patient, the intransitive subject, the possessed noun) unmarked. This is a unified dependent-marking principle operating at both the NP-internal and clause-internal levels.

5. **Confirm the absence of verbal agreement and reinforce case as the sole role-identifying mechanism.**
   Construct 2–3 sentences that make the invariance of verbs explicit. Use the same transitive verb with different agents and patients (different nouns, different numbers) and show that the verb form never changes. Optionally, use the same intransitive verb with different subjects. The solver's discovery: since verbs carry no person or number information, the only way to identify who is doing what to whom is through the case suffixes on the noun phrases. This makes the ergative/absolutive contrast from Stage 4 even more salient—the solver cannot "cheat" by looking at the verb and must rely entirely on NP morphology. This also reinforces the dependent-marking principle: the language's strategy for encoding grammatical relations is to mark the dependent/agentive role on the noun, not to agree the verb with its arguments.

6. **Construct contrastive and confirmatory sentences to force unique induction.**
   Build 3–4 sentences that eliminate alternative analyses:
   - A pair of sentences with the same possessed noun but different possessors (one singular, one plural), showing that the genitive suffix appears on the possessor in both cases and the possessed noun remains bare. This rules out the possibility that the suffix is somehow "on the possessed noun."
   - A transitive clause where the agent is plural (showing plural suffix + ergative suffix stacking) and the patient is singular and unmarked. This confirms that the ergative is a suffix on the agent, not a property of the verb or the patient.
   - A sentence combining a possessive NP as the agent of a transitive clause (e.g., "X's child saw Y"). This shows that the genitive-marked possessor can itself be embedded in a larger clause where the possessed noun (now the agent) bears the ergative suffix. The solver sees the full stacking: possessor-genitive + possessed-ergative + patient-absolutive + verb.
   - A sentence where a noun that was previously an unmarked intransitive subject now appears as a transitive patient (still unmarked) in a different sentence, confirming the S = P alignment.

7. **Construct maximally complex sentences combining three or more systems.**
   Build 2–3 sentences that layer multiple systems simultaneously:
   - A transitive clause where the agent is a possessive NP (genitive possessor + possessed noun with ergative suffix), the patient is a modified noun (adjective + noun), and the verb is invariant. This combines: dependent marking (genitive), modifier-before-noun (adjective before patient), ergative alignment (ergative on the possessed noun-as-agent), and no verbal agreement.
   - A sentence with a plural agent (plural + ergative stacking), a possessive NP as patient (genitive + bare possessed noun), and an adjective modifying the patient. This tests the solver's ability to parse stacked suffixes and maintain the head-final ordering even in complex NPs.
   - Optionally, a sentence with two clauses (linked by a simple conjunction or asyndetically) where one clause is transitive and the other is intransitive, showing the contrast between ergative-marked and absolutive-unmarked subjects across the two clauses.

8. **Design translation questions with progressive difficulty.**
   Create 6–8 questions of increasing complexity. Each question must test at least two of the core systems simultaneously (never a single rule in isolation):
   - Q1 (easy, target-to-English): A simple intransitive clause with a plural subject. Tests: plural suffix identification + no verbal agreement. The solver must recognize the plural and understand that the verb form tells them nothing about the subject.
   - Q2 (easy, target-to-English): A sentence with a possessive NP. Tests: dependent marking (identifying which element is the possessor via the genitive suffix) + modifier-before-noun order (the genitive possessor precedes the head).
   - Q3 (medium, target-to-English): A transitive clause with a modified patient. Tests: ergative-absolutive alignment (identifying A vs. P from case) + modifier-before-noun (adjective before patient noun) + no verbal agreement.
   - Q4 (medium, target-to-English): A transitive clause where the agent is a possessive NP. Tests: dependent marking (genitive on possessor) + ergative alignment (ergative on the possessed noun-as-agent) + modifier-before-noun (the genitive possessor precedes the possessed noun, which is the agent).
   - Q5 (medium-hard, English-to-target): A sentence requiring the solver to produce a possessive NP as the patient of a transitive clause. The solver must: place the genitive suffix on the possessor, leave the possessed noun bare (as patient/absolutive), place the ergative suffix on the agent, and order all modifiers before their heads.
   - Q6 (hard, English-to-target): A sentence requiring a plural agent (plural + ergative stacking), a possessive NP as patient (genitive + bare), and an adjective modifying the patient. The solver must correctly stack suffixes, maintain head-final ordering, and apply the ergative to the correct noun.
   - Q7 (hard, English-to-target): A two-clause sentence (or a single clause with multiple NPs) that requires the solver to apply all systems simultaneously: dependent marking, ergative alignment, modifier-before-noun, plural suffix, and the principle that verbs are invariant. The solver must produce a grammatically correct sentence that a Lezgian speaker would recognize as well-formed.

9. **Verify disambiguation and solvability.**
   - Confirm that in every possessive NP in the data, the possessor is the only element bearing a suffix and the possessed noun is morphologically bare. No sentence should allow the alternative analysis that the suffix belongs to the possessed noun.
   - Confirm that in every modifier-noun sequence, the modifier precedes the noun. No sentence should allow a noun-before-modifier reading.
   - Confirm that in every transitive clause, the ergative suffix appears on the agent and the patient is unmarked. The intransitive subject must also be unmarked, creating a clear S = P ≠ A pattern.
   - Confirm that no verb in the data shows any morphological variation (no person, number, aspect, or mood marking). Every verb token must be identical regardless of its arguments.
   - Confirm that the plural suffix is always present on plural nouns and absent on singular nouns, and that it is morphologically distinct from the genitive and ergative suffixes (different form, or at least a different position in the suffix stack).
   - Confirm that every rule tested by the questions is uniquely determined by at least one sentence in the data. No question should require a rule that the data does not establish.
   - Confirm that the two core features (dependent marking and modifier-before-noun order) are each tested in at least three questions, and that they are always tested in combination with at least one other rule (ergative alignment, plural, no verbal agreement), ensuring the solver must integrate multiple inferred rules simultaneously.
