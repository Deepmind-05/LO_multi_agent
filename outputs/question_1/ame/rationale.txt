

TARGET-LANGUAGE: Amele (Trans-New Guinea, Madang, Mabuso)

CONSTRUCTION RATIONALE

1. **Establish the morphological and lexical inventory.** Select a controlled vocabulary set comprising: (a) 8–12 countable nouns distributed across at least three semantic categories that will serve as the basis for possessive classification (e.g., a set of body-part terms, a set of kinship terms, and a set of natural-object or artifact terms); (b) 4–6 adjectives or stative modifiers that can postmodify these nouns; (c) 2–3 demonstratives; (d) one or two personal names to serve as unmarked possessor NPs; (e) a single conjunctional morpheme (call it C) that will serve multiple functions (see steps 7–8); (f) a set of possessive suffixes — at least three distinct allomorphs (label them abstractly as S₁, S₂, S₃ for planning purposes; the setter selects actual Amele forms). The critical design constraint: each possessive suffix must map to exactly one semantic class of possessed nouns, and no possessed noun in the vocabulary may be ambiguous between classes. This ensures the classification is uniquely recoverable.

2. **Define the structural templates explicitly.** The setter must commit to the following canonical patterns before constructing data:
   - **Attributive possessive NP:** [Possessor] [Possessed + Sₓ] [Modifier₁] [Modifier₂] …
     where Sₓ is the possessive suffix selected by the semantic class of the possessed noun; the possessor is morphologically bare (no suffix, no case marker); modifiers follow the possessed noun.
   - **Noun-modifier sequence (non-possessive):** [Noun] [Modifier₁] [Modifier₂] …
     where the noun is the head and all modifiers (adjectives, demonstratives, numerals, relative clauses) follow it.
   - **Predicative possession clause:** [X] C [Y]
     where C is the conjunctional morpheme and the clause means "X has Y" or "X and Y" (see step 8 for disambiguation).
   These templates are the ground truth. Every sentence in the puzzle must conform to one of them. The solver must recover them from the data.

3. **Construct the first evidence block: basic possessive NPs (2–3 sentences).** Present simple two-element possessive sequences of the form [Possessor] [Possessed + Sₓ]. Use a proper name as possessor in at least one example and a pronoun or kinship term as possessor in another. The possessed nouns in this block should all belong to the same semantic class (e.g., all body parts), so they all take the same suffix S₁. The solver's first inference: the element carrying the suffix is the possessed head; the bare element preceding it is the possessor. This establishes dependent marking (CORE 1). At this stage, the solver should not yet be able to determine the classification criterion, because only one class is visible.

4. **Construct the second evidence block: noun-modifier sequences (2–3 sentences).** Present non-possessive noun phrases where a noun is followed by one or two modifiers (an adjective and/or a demonstrative). The noun is the head; modifiers follow. The solver's inference: the language has consistent postnominal modification (CORE 2). This block should use nouns from different semantic categories (to avoid accidentally revealing the classification system yet) and should not include possessive suffixes, so the solver isolates the modifier-order pattern independently of the possessive system.

5. **Construct the third evidence block: combined possessive + modification (2–3 sentences).** Present possessive NPs where the possessed noun also carries a postnominal modifier: [Possessor] [Possessed + Sₓ] [Modifier]. This confirms that the element carrying the possessive suffix is the same element that precedes the modifier — i.e., it is the structural head of the entire NP. The solver now has two independent cues converging on head identification: (a) it carries the possessive suffix, and (b) it precedes its modifiers. This interaction (Interaction 3 in Agent 1) makes the head unambiguous even in longer NPs.

6. **Construct the fourth evidence block: possessive classification discovery (4–6 sentences).** This is the central difficulty layer. Present possessive NPs where the possessed nouns belong to different semantic classes, each taking a different suffix. The design principle: within each sentence, the possessed noun's semantic category should be transparently identifiable from its lexical meaning (e.g., "eye," "hand," "leg" are obviously body parts; "mother," "child," "sister" are obviously kinship; "river," "tree," "stone" are obviously natural objects). The solver must notice that the suffix is not uniform across all possessed nouns, hypothesize that the suffix is determined by the semantic class of the possessed noun, and identify the class boundaries. The evidence must contain at least three distinct classes, each represented by at least two different possessed nouns (so the solver can confirm the pattern is not idiosyncratic to a single noun). The possessor in these sentences should vary (proper names, kinship terms, pronouns) to confirm that the suffix depends on the possessed noun's class, not on the possessor's identity. The solver's inference: a possessive classification system exists; the criterion is semantic category of the head noun; the mapping from class to suffix is consistent.

7. **Construct the fifth evidence block: the conjunctional possessive (2–3 sentences).** Introduce a clause-level construction of the form [X] C [Y] that expresses "X has Y" (predicative possession). The critical design constraint: the conjunctional morpheme C must be the same form used in the attributive possessive NP as a linker or must be a form the solver has not yet encountered in a possessive context — the setter chooses. The key contrast to make visible: in the attributive construction, the possessed noun carries a suffix and the possessor is bare; in the predicative construction, neither X nor Y carries a possessive suffix, and the relationship is expressed by the conjunction C between them. The solver's inference: there are two distinct possessive strategies in Amele — dependent marking for attributive (NP-internal) possession and a conjunctional strategy for predicative (clause-level) possession. The solver must not overgeneralize the suffix pattern to the predicative construction. This is Interaction 2.

8. **Construct the sixth evidence block: disambiguation of the conjunctional morpheme (2–3 sentences).** If the WITH-language property (TARGET-SPECIFIC 3) is included, present sentences where C appears in a comitative/associative context ("X and Y" meaning "X together with Y") and in a predicative possession context ("X has Y"). The disambiguation must be resolvable from the data: provide at least one sentence where the comitative reading is semantically forced (e.g., two animate agents performing a joint action) and at least one where the possessive reading is forced (e.g., an inanimate object as Y, or a context where "having" is the only plausible interpretation). The solver's inference: C is polysemous (and/with/possessive), and the intended reading is determined by semantic and contextual cues. If the setter chooses not to include this layer, skip this step and use a distinct morpheme for the conjunctional possessive to avoid ambiguity.

9. **Construct the seventh evidence block: adjective/relative clause ambiguity (2 sentences, optional).** If the adjective/relative clause collapse (TARGET-SPECIFIC 4) is included, present postnominal modifiers where one is clearly adjectival (a simple stative property) and another is clearly a relative clause (contains a verb and an internal argument). Both follow the noun. The solver's inference: the formal position is the same (postnominal), but the internal structure differs — adjectives are monomorphemic or simple, while relative clauses contain verbal material. The solver uses internal structure, not position, to distinguish them. This adds a parsing layer within the NP without contradicting the noun-before-modifier rule.

10. **Construct the final evidence block: complex sentences combining 3+ systems (3–4 sentences).** These sentences should combine: (a) a possessive NP with a classified suffix + a postnominal modifier + a proper-noun possessor; (b) a predicative possession clause embedded or coordinated with another clause; (c) a relative clause modifying a possessed noun. Example structural template: [ProperName] [Possessed + S₂] [RelativeClause] [Verb] … or [X] C [Y + S₃] [Modifier] [Verb] … The purpose is to confirm that all rules interact compositionally and that the solver can parse multi-layered structures. These sentences should not introduce any new morphological or syntactic element not already established in steps 3–9.

11. **Design the translation questions with progressive difficulty.** The question set should test the concepts in increasing combinations:
   - **Q1 (easy, target→English):** A simple possessive NP with a body-part possessed noun (S₁ class) and a proper-noun possessor. Tests: dependent marking + head identification.
   - **Q2 (easy, target→English):** A noun-modifier sequence (noun + adjective + demonstrative). Tests: postnominal modification.
   - **Q3 (medium, target→English):** A possessive NP where the possessed noun belongs to a class the solver has seen in the data but not in the exact same configuration (e.g., a kinship possessed noun with a modifier). Tests: classification + dependent marking + modification.
   - **Q4 (medium, target→English):** A predicative possession clause. Tests: recognition of the conjunctional strategy as distinct from dependent marking.
   - **Q5 (medium-hard, English→target):** A possessive NP where the possessed noun belongs to a class the solver has identified but must select the correct suffix for. The possessor is a new proper name not seen in the data. Tests: productive application of classification + dependent marking.
   - **Q6 (hard, English→target):** A sentence combining a possessive NP (with a classified suffix and a postnominal modifier) inside a larger clause that also contains a predicative possession construction. Tests: all three possessive/modification systems simultaneously.
   - **Q7 (hard, English→target):** A sentence requiring the solver to choose between the attributive and predicative strategies based on the syntactic context (NP-internal vs. clause-level), and to apply the correct suffix. Tests: functional split between the two possessive strategies.
   - **Q8 (hardest, English→target, optional):** If the WITH-language property is included, a sentence where the solver must determine whether C is being used comitatively or possessively based on semantic context, and construct the sentence accordingly. Tests: polysemy disambiguation + productive application.

12. **Verify unambiguous inference and solvability.** Before finalizing the puzzle, the setter must check:
   - (a) In every attributive possessive NP in the data, the possessor is morphologically bare and the possessed noun carries exactly one possessive suffix. No sentence should allow an analysis where the suffix is on the possessor.
   - (b) In every noun-modifier sequence, the head noun is unambiguously identifiable. If a noun could be confused with a modifier (e.g., a kinship term that could be either possessor or possessed), ensure that the possessive suffix or the modifier position resolves the ambiguity.
   - (c) The possessive classification is uniquely determined: for each suffix, the set of possessed nouns that take it forms a semantically coherent class, and no possessed noun in the data takes a suffix inconsistent with its class.
   - (d) The conjunctional possessive is structurally distinct from the attributive possessive in every example: no sentence should be parseable as both.
   - (e) If the WITH-language property is included, every instance of C in the data has a uniquely determined interpretation from context. No sentence should be genuinely ambiguous.
   - (f) Every rule required by the questions is supported by at least one sentence in the data. No question should require the solver to apply a rule that has no evidential basis.
   - (g) The two CORE concepts (dependent marking, noun-before-modifier order) are each tested in at least three questions and are always tested in combination with at least one other concept (never in isolation).
